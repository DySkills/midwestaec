# Cite the reports, not the web pages

midwestaec.com can build a free, reusable source library for every topic on its list (basement moisture, frost, walls and vapor, attics and ice dams, insulation, air sealing, heat pumps, humidity, radon, frozen pipes, roofing, windows and mold) from a few dozen documents in five layers. The layers are: federal lab reports held in permanent repositories (OSTI, Treesearch, EPA document numbers); Building Science Corporation's free digests for the physics; Canadian government research for zones 6A and 7; Midwest extension fact sheets for regional practice; and Midwest state agencies and nonprofits for local data and field results. Where a source lives matters more in 2026 than it did two years ago. In July 2026 DOE deleted more than 1,600 Energy Saver pages, but the lab reports behind them stayed online, and in September 2026 DOE named PNNL's Building America Solution Center (BASC) as the publication venue for a new program. So the rule is to cite the report by its identifier (a DOI or document number) and treat agency web pages as disposable. The region's strongest institutions are the University of Minnesota (Extension and its Cold Climate Housing Program), the Center for Energy and Environment in Minneapolis, Slipstream in Madison, K-State's radon program, Purdue's Herrick Laboratories, NDSU and MU Extension, and the Minnesota Departments of Commerce and Health. Getting cited by them is realistic but slow. Their linking policies screen out pages that sell or carry intrusive ads, and Google gives .edu and .gov links no special ranking weight. The payoff is credibility and referral traffic, and the route is ad-free reference pages, accurate expert interviews and real help with their programs. Asking for links does not work. Many entries below, especially for windows, roofing and frozen pipes and for most states outside Minnesota, are unverified leads rather than confirmed sources. Each entry is marked so the owner knows what to open before citing it.

**How to read the status marks.** **[V]** means the page or document was opened or confirmed live during research on 16–19 November 2026. **[L]** means a lead from researchers' background knowledge or a search snippet only: the owner must find and open it before it goes into a source pack. Even a [V] entry needs the owner's own check on the day of use, because 2025–2026 showed that federal pages can vanish within weeks.

## Federal pages vanished in 2026 while their reports survived

### What changed, and what it means for citations

The removals followed a clear pattern. On or before 3 July 2026, DOE removed **more than 1,600 pages** from the Energy Saver section of energy.gov, including the do-it-yourself and professional home energy assessment pages and the thermostat guidance. It did not answer press questions ([Government Executive](https://www.govexec.com/management/2026/07/energy-department-removes-more-1600-webpages-public-website/414879/)). Over the same months the technical layer survived and was even renewed. On 10–11 September 2026, DOE launched "Building America New Construction Support" and said findings from each engagement "will be summarized and published on the Building America Solution Center." The press release came from DOE's **Office of Critical Minerals and Energy Innovation (CMEI)**, not EERE, so energy.gov/eere/ addresses should be expected to move ([DOE CMEI](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/articles/doe-launches-new-program-help-builders-cut-construction-costs-and-save-americans); [BASC BANCS page](https://basc.pnnl.gov/bancs)). Budgets are falling but have not been zeroed. The FY2026 appropriation cut the Building Technologies Office about 13%, and ENERGY STAR received at least $33 million but is "operating with limited staff and function" ([Utility Dive](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/energy-star-gets-full-2026-funding-from-congress/811087/)). NREL is now the **National Laboratory of the Rockies**, after layoffs of at least 114 staff in May 2025 and 134 in February 2026 ([CPR News](https://www.cpr.org/2026/02/09/nrel-layoffs-2026/)). The administration proposed eliminating EPA's radon program in FY2026; the House bill cut it 49% and the Senate bill held it roughly level ([AARST](https://aarst.org/capitol-hill-update-august-2025/)). This research did not find the final enacted radon figure. One piece of good news for wood and moisture topics: the USDA Forest Products Laboratory in Madison was explicitly kept open in the Forest Service's September 2026 review, which closes 23 other research sites ([Wood Central](https://woodcentral.com.au/forest-products-laboratory-retained/)).

The lesson for the library is direct. Homeowner explainer pages are the least stable federal content. Numbered reports in permanent repositories are the most stable. A citation to "PNNL-33270, DOI 10.2172/1893981" will still resolve when energy.gov has been reorganized twice more ([OSTI 1893981](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1893981)).

### The durable-link rule, in fallback order

For any federal source, the owner should cite the most permanent identifier available. The first choice is a report DOI or repository record: OSTI (doi.org/10.2172/…), USDA Treesearch (research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/…), NIST Technical Series (DOI 10.6028/…) [L], or DTIC/ERDC for Army CRREL reports [L]. The second is an EPA document number with an epa.gov link, plus the matching copy on EPA's own frozen snapshot sites (19january2017snapshot, 19january2021snapshot and 19january2025snapshot.epa.gov) [V] ([EPA 2025 snapshot example](https://19january2025snapshot.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/moisture-control-guidance-building-design-construction-and-maintenance-0)). The third is a BASC library page, which also indexes other agencies' documents such as the EPA Moisture Control Guidance and the FPL Wood Handbook ([BASC entry](https://basc.pnnl.gov/library/moisture-control-guidance-building-design-construction-and-maintenance)). The fourth is a dated Wayback Machine snapshot or End of Term Web Archive copy for a page that has already been removed ([eotarchive.org](https://eotarchive.org/)). Government Executive's coverage gives working Wayback paths for removed Energy Saver pages, such as web.archive.org/web/20260607180914/https://www.energy.gov/energysaver/do-it-yourself-home-energy-assessments. The fifth is a GPO govinfo or FDLP permanent URL; one FDLP PURL appeared in coverage of the deletion, but its contents were not checked ([purl.fdlp.gov](https://purl.fdlp.gov/GPO/gpo126468)).

The same logic applies to Canada and the universities. publications.gc.ca and the Library and Archives Canada web archive have hosted CMHC and NRC documents at stable addresses for more than 15 years, while live CMHC pages have been restructured repeatedly. The UMN Conservancy, Purdue e-Pubs and UNL DigitalCommons give handle-style addresses that outlast extension website redesigns ([UMN Conservancy item](https://conservancy.umn.edu/items/af092c48-2b09-4276-8dbf-bd2dbf4d4330); [UNL DigitalCommons](https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/extensionhist/1106/)).

The workflow addition is the researchers' inference rather than a sourced practice. For every source in a pack, the owner should record the persistent identifier, the URL actually used, the access date and an archive snapshot. The owner should also keep a private copy of the PDF for re-verification, without republishing it. The weekly external-link check from the earlier plan then becomes an early warning: when a cited agency page returns 404, the record already holds the replacement link.

### Citation formats that survive URL changes

| Source type | Format the article's reference line should use |
|---|---|
| OSTI lab report | Authors, "Title," Lab report no. (e.g. PNNL-37127), year, doi.org/10.2172/NNNNNNN |
| BASC guide | U.S. DOE Building America Solution Center, "Guide title," PNNL, basc.pnnl.gov/…, accessed date |
| EPA guidance | EPA, "Title," EPA document no. (e.g. 402-F-13053), year, epa.gov URL plus snapshot URL |
| BSC digest | Lstiburek, J., "BSD-106: Understanding Vapor Barriers," Building Science Corporation, page effective date, HTML page URL |
| Canadian Building Digest | Author, "Title," CBD-xx, National Research Council of Canada, year, archive URL |
| CMHC fact sheet | CMHC, "Title," About Your House, catalogue no. NHxx-xx, year, publications.gc.ca URL |
| Extension fact sheet | Authors, "Title," publication no., university Extension, "reviewed" year, URL |

For Building Science Corporation, use the page's effective date and not the PDF file name: BSD-106 carries an effective date of 15 April 2011, while its PDF is named "2013" ([BSC BSD-106](https://buildingscience.com/documents/digests/bsd-106-understanding-vapor-barriers)). For extension fact sheets, record the "reviewed" year, since many are older documents that have been re-reviewed rather than new research.

## A five-layer library covers all thirteen topics

### Layering sources so physics leads and code follows

The owner's editorial choice of physics first, climate zone second and code as a note maps onto a credibility hierarchy that the research supports. It runs from field and lab data, to standards (cited by number and edition), to practitioner research reports, to expert opinion, to industry material (disclosed as such). Each article's source pack should hold one source per layer where possible. These are: a physics anchor that explains the mechanism (BSC digest, EPA Moisture Control Guidance, Canadian Building Digest); a cold-climate practice source that says what works in zones 5A–7 (UMN Extension, CMHC, a weatherization field guide); a Midwest data source (state climatology, state radon data, CEE or Slipstream field results); and a code note naming the model-code section and edition, with the state caveat from the owner's state code sheet.

Climate zones need their own rule. The authoritative federal reference is PNNL-33270, "Guide to Determining Climate Zone by County: Building America and IECC 2021 Updates" (2022). BASC provides GIS shapefiles for both the pre-2021 and 2021 maps ([OSTI 1893981](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1893981); [BASC data files](https://basc.pnnl.gov/guide-determining-climate-zone-county-data-files)) [V]. The 2021 map moved many counties into warmer zones, and Illinois' Smart Energy Design Assistance Center summarizes the residential changes from the 2018 to the 2021 IECC ([SEDAC PDF](https://smartenergy.illinois.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/2021-IECC-Updates-Residential.pdf)) [V]. A county's zone can therefore differ by map edition, and an AI draft should never assign a zone from memory. The owner can build a 12-state county-to-zone table from the BASC shapefiles once. That table would also be the kind of original, citable data asset the earlier plan recommended.

The code layer stays secondary because the numbers really do diverge by edition. BASC's side-by-side table shows a ceiling minimum of R-49 under the 2012–2018 IECC and R-60 under the 2021 IECC in zones 4–7 ([BASC](https://basc.pnnl.gov/information/2009-2021-iecc-and-irc-minimum-insulation-requirements-new-homes)), and the region's states are spread across those editions.

### The core library, cluster by cluster

**Water, soil and foundations.** The physics anchor for every moisture article is EPA's *Moisture Control Guidance for Building Design, Construction and Maintenance* (EPA 402-F-13053, December 2013). It covers liquid water, capillarity, air leakage and vapor diffusion for foundations, walls and roofs, and it exists in EPA's 2017 and 2025 snapshots and on BASC, which makes it the most redundantly archived document in the library ([2017 snapshot PDF](https://19january2017snapshot.epa.gov/sites/production/files/2014-08/documents/moisture-control.pdf)) [V]. For Midwest practice, NDSU Extension's basement series is current and flood-focused: DE1570 "Dealing with continuing basement seepage," AE1594 "Preparing flood-damaged homes for winter," and an August 2025 release on drying procedures ([DE1570](https://www.ag.ndsu.edu/publications/disasters/dealing-with-continuing-basement-seepage/de1570.pdf/view); [NDSU 2025](https://www.ag.ndsu.edu/news/newsreleases/2025/august/safety-and-efficient-drying-procedures-are-key-to-basement-water-damage-recovery)) [V]. CMHC's *Green Basements* and *Basement Flood Prevention* guides fit zones 6A–7 well, but the copies found were hosted by a British Columbia program site and InspectApedia, so the owner should locate a publications.gc.ca or CMHC library copy before citing ([CMHC library](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/library)). Frost articles get strong local data from Minnesota's State Climatology Office, whose recurring "Frost Depth in Minnesota" journal entries (winter 2025, 2018, 2003) document real penetration ([MN DNR 2025](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/climate/journal/frost-depth-minnesota-winter-2025.html)) [V], and from the NWS North Central River Forecast Center frost-depth map ([NWS](https://www.weather.gov/ncrfc/LMI_FrostDepthMap)) [V]. Minnesota's Department of Labor and Industry "frost tag notes" document code-side frost protection for footings ([MN DLI](https://www.dli.mn.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/frost-tag-notes090121.pdf)) [V]. Sinkhole and settlement content can cite MN DNR's karst report GW-01 ([MN DNR](https://files.dnr.state.mn.us/waters/groundwater_section/mapping/gw/gw01_report.pdf)) [V]. Equivalent climatology and geological-survey products for the other 11 states were not checked, and they are among the most valuable leads to verify. The physics of frost heave comes from the Canadian Building Digests, CBD-1 to CBD-250, published 1960–1990. A George Brown College research guide updated 10 September 2026 indexes them ([CBD guide](https://researchguides.georgebrown.ca/CBD)) [V]. The specific frost-heave digest numbers were not confirmed.

**The enclosure: walls, vapor, insulation, air sealing.** Building Science Corporation's digests are the backbone. They are free to read without login in 2026, and the confirmed core set is BSD-104 (air barriers), BSD-105 (drainage planes), BSD-106 (vapor barriers), BSD-138 (moisture and materials), BSD-018 (the building enclosure), BSCInfo 311 (vapor-open assemblies) and conference paper CP-1101 on low-permeance vapor barriers ([BSC digest listing](https://buildingscience.com/bsc-document-type/building-science-digests?page=4); [BSCInfo 311](https://buildingscience.com/sites/default/files/migrate/pdf/BSCInfo_311_Vapor_Open_Assemblies.pdf); [CP-1101](https://www.buildingscience.com/documents/confpapers/cp-1101-influence-low-permeance-vapor-barriers-roof-wall-performance/view)) [V]. BSC's "Inward Vapor Drive" and "Outward Vapor Drive" Building America documents sit on an ORNL server, and BSD-106 is mirrored on BASC, which gives backup links ([ORNL hyperion](https://hyperion.ornl.gov/pub/bsa/InwardVaporDrive.pdf); [BASC mirror](https://basc.pnnl.gov/library/understanding-vapor-barriers)) [V]. BSC must be labeled honestly. It is a consulting firm, its "Insight" columns are deliberately polemical, and some positions, such as its stance against interior polyethylene and its view of unvented roof risk, are contested. Articles should present these as expert positions and pair them with field data. The Midwest complement is University of Minnesota work: the DOE-funded "Affordable High-R Panel Wall" project and the NorthernSTAR 1.5-story demonstration house report on OSTI ([DOE project](https://www.energy.gov/eere/buildings/articles/affordable-high-r-panel-wall-system-demonstration); [OSTI 2204246](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/2204246)) [V]. The DOE project page sits on an energy.gov/eere address, so the owner should capture an archive copy now. For wood moisture content, decay and sheathing, the *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-282, 2021) is free by chapter ([Treesearch 62200](https://research.fs.usda.gov/treesearch/62200)) [V]. Hygrothermal modeling claims can cite the WUFI-ORNL/IBP documentation on OSTI ([OSTI](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/1231793)) [V].

The most underused practical sources in the region are the state Weatherization Assistance Program field guides. They are free, current, detailed state technical standards on air sealing, insulation, moisture and combustion safety. The confirmed ones are Wisconsin's PY26-27 field guide, the Minnesota online field guide with a policy manual updated 1 July 2026, and Michigan's 2018 Standard Work Specifications ([WI PY26-27](https://energyandhousing.wi.gov/Documents/Weatherization/PY26-27%20Weatherization%20Field%20Guide_FINAL.pdf); [MN field guide](https://wxfieldguide.com/mn/MNWxFg/Title/Title.htm); [MI field guide](https://waynemetro.org/wp-content/uploads/Michigan-Weatherization-Field-Guide-Standard-Work-Specifications-2018-1.pdf)) [V]. Minnesota Commerce's *Home Energy Guide* (July 2025) is the best single homeowner-level state publication. It covers R-values, air sealing, ice dams, vapor retarders, ventilation, heat pumps and combustion safety ([MN Home Energy Guide](https://mn.gov/commerce-stat/pdfs/home-energy-guide.pdf)) [V].

**Roof and attic.** The UMN Extension page "Dealing with and preventing ice dams" (authors including Patrick Huelman, reviewed 2022) is the regional anchor. It attributes ice dams to uneven roof temperatures from heat loss, names air leakage through ceiling penetrations as often the main cause, puts air sealing before insulation, and does not recommend mechanical attic ventilation as an ice-dam fix in Minnesota ([UMN Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/garden-and-home/home-maintenance/caring-for-a-home/dealing-with-and-preventing-ice-dams)) [V]. It pairs with the historic bulletin in the UMN Conservancy, NDSU's ice-dam pages, Wisconsin Focus on Energy's ice-dam fact sheet, and the Extension Disaster Education Network's collection ([NDSU](https://www.ndsu.edu/agriculture/extension/check-your-roof-ice-dams); [Focus on Energy PDF](https://assets.focusonenergy.com/staging/inline-files/2023/RES-IAS-Ice_Dams-Fact_Sheet.pdf); [EDEN](https://resources.extensiondisaster.net/resources/ice-dams-ca69bea1)) [V]. Roofing beyond ice dams is thin in free, physics-grade sources. The NRCA Roofing Manual is paid, Professional Roofing articles are free but practitioner-level, and ORNL's roof research and calculators were not checked. Roofing articles should therefore lean on attic-physics sources plus manufacturer installation instructions, disclosed as industry material.

**Mechanical systems and indoor air.** For cold-climate heat pumps, the single most citable federal document is **PNNL-37127**, "Performance Results from DOE Cold Climate Heat Pump Challenge Field Validation" (January 2025). It covers prototype units field-tested in real homes from 2022 to 2024 ([PNNL](https://www.pnnl.gov/publications/performance-results-doe-cold-climate-heat-pump-challenge-field-validation)) [V], with a companion OSTI record ([OSTI 1973090](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1973090)). The researchers did not extract its performance figures, so the owner must read the PDF before quoting any temperature or efficiency number. Midwest field evidence comes from CEE's CARD-funded projects on heat pump heating loads, ducts and cold-weather operation, from Slipstream's ductless minisplit integration-controls field test, and from Purdue's Herrick conference papers ([CEE CARD grants](https://www.mncee.org/cee-card-grants-furthering-our-research-help-minnesotans-save); [Slipstream field test](https://slipstreaminc.org/publications/ductless-heat-pumps-integration-controls-field-study); [Purdue CHPB](https://chpb.engineering.purdue.edu/herrick-conferences)) [V]. ACCA's sizing manuals are paid standards that should be cited by name and edition, not quoted. ASHRAE offers free read-only standards behind an account, but this research could not confirm whether 62.2 is on the 2026 list ([ASHRAE read-only](https://www.ashrae.org/technical-resources/standards-and-guidelines/read-only-versions-of-ashrae-standards)). For mold, EPA's *A Brief Guide to Mold, Moisture and Your Home* and the EPA Mold Course are live ([EPA Brief Guide](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home)) [V]. Minnesota Department of Health's Healthy Homes cluster covers testing, asthma triggers and indoor contaminants from one office ([MDH Testing Homes](https://www.health.mn.gov/communities/environment/healthyhomes/testing.html)) [V]. For indoor humidity, the UMN-origin "Home Moisture" guide is still republished nationally ([TN DOH copy](https://tennessee.gov/content/dam/tn/health/documents/healthy-places/healthy-homes/Home_Moisture_Minnesota.pdf)) [V]. The original UMN copy should be located and cited in its place.

**Radon.** Radon is the topic most exposed to federal cuts and best served by state and university sources. K-State Engineering Extension runs the Kansas Radon Program and the National Radon Program Services site, sosradon.org, which posted EPA Region 7 stakeholder material in March 2026 ([sosradon.org](https://sosradon.org/node/32)) [V]. K-State recommends mitigation at **2.7 pCi/L, below EPA's 4 pCi/L action level** ([K-State FAQ](https://engext.ksu.edu/node/208)) [V]. Articles should present both numbers and attribute each one. MU Extension guide G1968 adds a second Midwest .edu voice ([MU G1968](https://extension.missouri.edu/publications/g1968)) [V]. The American Lung Association's 2022 testing-disparity reports for Wisconsin, Iowa, Minnesota and Illinois summarize state test data by county or ZIP code ([ALA Minnesota](https://www.lung.org/getmedia/5e0b9c33-8bed-499a-893a-61784fc1991e/Radon-Testing-Disparity-report-for-Minnesota-2022-08-14.pdf)) [V]. Commercial radon aggregators should not be cited as primary sources.

### Independent and Canadian sources: what is free and how to weight it

Canada matters because southern Ontario and Quebec climates fall roughly in zones 5A–6A and the Prairies in 6A–7, which is the researchers' mapping and is unverified. The Canadian Building Digests supply durable physics but date from 1960–1990, so cite them for principles, never for products or code. CMHC's library offers more than 25,000 digital items, and its *About Your House* fact sheets survive as catalogued PDFs on publications.gc.ca (for example NH15-72-2007 and the NH18-24 series) ([CMHC library](https://www.cmhc-schl.gc.ca/library); [NH15-72-2007](https://publications.gc.ca/collections/collection_2017/schl-cmhc/NH15-72-2007-eng.pdf)) [V]. The National Building Code of Canada has been free electronically since 2021, which is useful for "Canada's model code requires…" comparisons but never as a US rule ([NRC Codes Canada](https://nrc.canada.ca/en/certifications-evaluations-standards/codes-canada/codes-canada-publications)) [V]. The Alaska-based Cold Climate Housing Research Center became NREL's Alaska Campus in 2020, and no current publications index was found ([NREL Alaska](https://nrel.gov/about/alaska.html)).

RDH Building Science's technical library filters by "Cold Climate", but its free-download status was not confirmed ([RDH](https://rdh.com/technical-library/)). Fine Homebuilding and Green Building Advisor are partly paywalled ([FHB memberships](https://finehomebuilding.com/memberships); [GBA](https://greenbuildingadvisor.com/question/everything-at-gba-is-green-pro)). They are useful for field technique and expert interpretation, but readers cannot verify a claim behind a paywall, so the free primary source should be cited wherever one makes the same point. For peer-reviewed papers, Unpaywall and Open Access Helper find legally posted author copies ([University at Buffalo Libraries](https://research.lib.buffalo.edu/articleaccess/unpaywall)). The citation should be the journal version of record, and the open copy should be linked only when it is a repository or author posting. Shadow libraries are off-limits for a commercial site. Industry associations (APA, NAIMA, cellulose and spray-foam groups, ABAA) publish free, data-based material that has to be disclosed as industry-sourced. None of their access terms were verified.

## Twelve states, thirty-plus programs: who is good for what

### Universities and extension

Minnesota is the region's strongest single source, but its substance sits in different places than its program names suggest. The Cold Climate Housing Program in UMN's Bioproducts and Biosystems Engineering department still lists a principal investigator and sets out ten cold-climate house principles, but it has no publication list ([UMN BBE](https://bbe.umn.edu/research/cold-climate-housing-program)) [V]. The citable work lives in Extension fact sheets, the Conservancy repository and OSTI. Elsewhere, Extension housing pages are mostly older fact sheets that are periodically reviewed rather than new research. The exceptions are NDSU (basement water, floods, ice dams), MU Extension (a housing hub with news dated 14 October 2026, plus radon and the Healthy Homes Partnership legacy) and Purdue's Herrick Laboratories, whose July 2026 conferences carried **more than 340 papers** ([MU Extension Housing](https://extension.missouri.edu/topics/housing); [IIR news](https://iifiir.org/en/news/2026-herrick-conferences-deliver-a-week-of-high-impact-research-and-global-collaboration)) [V].

## Earning .edu and .gov references means passing their linking rules

### What the policies actually screen for

The barrier is institutional policy, not the quality of the site's writing. The University of North Carolina Wilmington's web guide, a representative example from outside the region, allows links to commercial organizations only when they serve the university, acknowledge support, or sit in "a compilation of hyperlinks that provide reference information" under a disclaimer. It states that "advertising and links to pages that directly sell products are not permitted" ([UNCW](https://redirectapp.uncw.edu/myuncw/about/brand/web-social-media/web-guide/links-commercial-websites)). EPA's external-link procedure requires that linked sites support its mission and "reflect high standards in quality," and it excludes sites that "unreasonably inconvenience the user," naming multiple pop-up advertisements as an example ([EPA](https://epa.gov/web-policies-and-procedures/procedure-external-site-links)). In the Midwest, SDSU Extension states that it does "not recommend or endorse any specific tests, products, procedures, opinions, professionals," and bars commercial use of its materials without written consent ([SDSU Extension Legal](https://extension.sdstate.edu/legal)). A page whose purpose ends in a contractor lead form sits uncomfortably close to endorsing "professionals". The one confirmed Midwest example of a state publication linking beyond government shows the realistic ceiling. Minnesota Commerce's *Home Energy Guide* links to ENERGY STAR, state housing sites and the nonprofit CEE, and to no commercial site ([MN Home Energy Guide](https://mn.gov/commerce-stat/pdfs/home-energy-guide.pdf)).

The SEO expectation also needs resetting. Google has said for years that a .edu or .gov address gives a link no extra weight. In 2018 John Mueller said that because of the misconception, ".edu links… get link-spammed quite a bit… we ignore a ton of the links on those sites" ([Search Engine Roundtable](https://www.seroundtable.com/google-ignores-a-lot-of-edu-links-26308.html)). Buying or bartering links, including scholarship and discount-page schemes, violates Google's link-spam policy unless the links are marked sponsored or nofollow. That reading comes from a secondary summary and should be checked against Google's own page ([eSEOspace summary](https://eseospace.com/blog/google-link-spam-policy/)). Google ran confirmed spam updates on 24 March, 24 June and 18 August 2026 ([TechWyse](https://www.techwyse.com/?p=77933)). The real value of a citation from UMN Extension or a state radon program is that a homeowner trusts the page more and clicks through from a trusted source. This research found **no documented case** of a small independent home site earning such citations, so the plan below is built from policy logic and science-journalism practice, not a proven playbook.

### Make a citable reference layer first

Before any outreach, the owner should create something that can pass these screens: a /library/ or /research/ section of reference pages, one per topic cluster. These would carry no Networx form, minimal or no affiliate links, full citations with identifiers, the climate-zone table, an author and method statement, a "last verified" date and a corrections policy. Each page would link onward to the monetized article and never the reverse. This is an inference from the UNCW and EPA criteria, and no evidence shows it raises link rates, but it is the only configuration that fits the "reference compilation" category those policies allow. Two rules protect the relationships the plan depends on. An expert's name, quote or university logo must never appear next to a lead form or product box, because that looks like endorsement and breaks university commercial-use rules. Extension material should be summarized and cited rather than republished, which Illinois Extension's copyright and attribution guide and SDSU's consent requirement both support ([Illinois Extension guide](https://extension.illinois.edu/sites/default/files/2021_copyright_and_attribution_best_practices_guide.pdf)).

### Partnership models ranked by realism

Six models are open to a one-person site. They are ranked here by the researchers' judgment, and none was tested with a Midwest institution.

The most realistic is the **expert interview**. The owner contacts the named author of a specific fact sheet, such as a UMN Extension housing page or an NDSU basement publication, through the institution's public routes: UMN Extension's contact page and "Ask a question" form, MU Extension's specialist directory, or a college media-relations office ([UMN Extension contact](https://extension.umn.edu/about-extension/contact-us); [MU Extension people](https://extension.missouri.edu/people); [K-State Media Guide](https://www.k-state.edu/news/media/)). Extension staff often route media requests through communications offices, so copying that office is legitimate ([MSU media response guide](https://www.canr.msu.edu/od/uploads/files/Strategic_Connections_and_Communications/Media_Response.pdf)).

Second is the **"what the research says" summary**. The owner writes an accurate explainer of an open report, such as a CEE or Slipstream field study or PNNL-37127, and sends it to the authoring organization for an accuracy check of the passages describing its work.

Third is **helping their programs reach homeowners**. This is the most concrete offer available in 2026. CEE is publicly recruiting homeowners outside the Twin Cities for its heat pump heating-load and duct study ([CEE research participants](https://www.mncee.org/become-research-participant); [CEE project](https://www.mncee.org/heating-load-estimates-and-duct-performance-diagnostics-successful-ashp-retrofit-installations)). Radon programs run test-kit campaigns, especially in January, which MSU Extension and others mark as National Radon Action Month. Promoting these, without asking for anything back, is useful to the institution and gives a reason to stay in touch.

Fourth is **original data offered under an open license**. Examples are the 12-state county climate-zone table, a compiled radon-program directory with current URLs, or anonymized reader reports of ice dams by county.

Fifth, and longer-term, is a **student project** arranged through a faculty member, with no expectation of a link.

Last is **sponsorship**. It runs through development offices and yields at most an acknowledgement link, which should be marked sponsored. A formal agreement with a state agency, such as the MOU or grant relationship that EPA treats as an exception, is unrealistic for a monetized one-person site.

### The outreach email and what to leave out

The Open Notebook's guidance, drawn from science journalists and the scientists they contact, sets the format ([The Open Notebook](https://www.theopennotebook.com/2021/09/14/please-dont-ignore-me-requesting-interviews-with-scientists/)). The subject line reads "Media inquiry:" plus outlet and topic. The salutation is formal. One sentence introduces the outlet with a link, and one introduces the author, which matters most for small, hard-to-find outlets. The email asks specific questions and gives a time window with a time zone. A single follow-up after about a week is normal, since silence usually means the email was buried. For this site, the email also needs a monetization disclosure, because the institution's endorsement rules make it material.

> **Subject:** Media inquiry: midwestaec.com – your [year] fact sheet on [topic]
> Dear Dr./Prof./Mr./Ms. [Name],
> I write midwestaec.com, a site that explains building physics to Midwest homeowners by climate zone, citing primary research [link]. About me and how I verify claims: [About link]. The site is supported by contractor-referral forms and affiliate links; experts are not paid and are not presented as endorsing any product or contractor, and quotes never appear beside referral forms.
> I'm writing about [specific question your publication raises, e.g., whether air sealing guidance changes for 1.5-story homes]. Two or three questions: [Q1] [Q2] [Q3].
> Could we talk for 15–20 minutes by phone, video or email between [dates], [time zone]? I'll send any passages that quote or describe your work for an accuracy check before publishing. Corrections policy: [link].

The researchers also set out what to leave out. Never ask for a link, a link swap or a "share." After publication, send a one-line courtesy note with the URL and nothing more; if the page is useful, staff can add it to a resource list on their own. Avoid scholarship pages, discount pages, paid placements on student or faculty sites, and widgets or infographics with embedded links. Do not imply an affiliation the site lacks. When writing to librarians who maintain LibGuides, frame the page as a free reference with no paywall. Library selection criteria emphasize authority, accuracy and currency, and libraries route vendor pitches through a separate solicitation process ([Purdue LibGuide practices](https://guides.lib.purdue.edu/creatingalibguide/bestpractices/content); [FAU vendor solicitations](https://libguides.fau.edu/collectionselectors/vendor-solicitations)). Academic timing is an inference rather than a sourced finding: avoid finals weeks and semester starts, and expect housing specialists to have more time in summer.

### A sequence for the first year

The outreach plan runs in four steps, and each depends on the one before it. **First, in months one and two,** build the reference layer for the three strongest clusters (basement moisture, ice dams and attics, cold-climate heat pumps). Cite at least one Midwest .edu or .gov source per page, and work through the verification queue below for those clusters. **Second, in months two to four,** start cite-and-notify: when a published article accurately uses a UMN, NDSU, MU, K-State, CEE or Slipstream source, send the courtesy note. Send two or three interview requests a month, each tied to a specific publication. **Third, around the seasonal peaks,** run program-promotion offers: radon in January for K-State, MU and the state radon programs; ice dams in early winter for UMN and NDSU; basement water in spring for NDSU. This is also when local media cite sources, as the earlier plan noted. **Fourth, from month six,** publish the open climate-zone and radon-program data assets and offer them to MEEA, CEE, the state energy offices and Focus on Energy as free references. The realistic first-year result is a handful of expert quotes and relationships, with links as an occasional by-product. Nothing in this research supports a firmer forecast.

## Conclusion

The 2025–2026 removals reversed an old assumption: the agency web page used to be the safe, authoritative citation, and the PDF was the obscure one. Now the numbered report in OSTI, the EPA document number with its snapshot, the Treesearch handbook and the publications.gc.ca fact sheet are the stable layer, and explainer pages are what disappears. A library built on identifiers is also exactly what an AI-first-draft workflow needs. A source pack of DOIs and document numbers is easy to check, hard for a model to fabricate convincingly, and simple to re-verify when the weekly link check flags a dead URL.

The second shift concerns what the owner should expect from universities and agencies. Their linking rules exclude the monetized pages and admit the reference pages, so the source library is also the outreach strategy. The same ad-free, fully cited cluster pages that discipline the AI drafts are the only pages a UMN, K-State or Minnesota Commerce editor could link to under their own policies. Midwest research in 2026 is concentrated in a small, interconnected network (CEE, Slipstream, UMN, Purdue, MN Commerce funding), with a DOE demonstration running through 2028. Accurate, repeated coverage of that network's open results is the most plausible way for a one-person site to become known to it.
