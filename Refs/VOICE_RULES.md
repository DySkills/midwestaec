# Voice rules (learned from Dylen's edits of the pilot article)

Source: Dylen's first-half edits of the February thaw pilot, November 24, 2026. Add to this file each time an edit shows a new pattern. These sit on top of AGENT_BRIEFING.md and EDITORIAL_GUIDE.md.

## Talk to the reader who is searching right now
- Write in second person, to someone standing under the drip. Example from the edit: "if you are here from searching what is happening right now, you know what the problem is."
- Open on what the reader sees and does ("No rain, low humidity, and a call to a roofer."), not on a staged scene with named cities.

## Headings are the reader's own questions
- "How Did Water Get into the Attic?", "Frost or Roof Leak?", "Frost or Ice Dam?"
- Short, plain, ending in a question mark where it fits.

## Explain physics with an everyday comparison
- "Heated air moves upward like cool water flows downward."
- "Your house may feel like a cocoon from the cold, but it's not isolated from the world of building physics."
- One comparison per idea. The physics claim still has to be correct.

## Practical, low-effort instructions
- Tell the reader the easiest way to check: "a quick climb up the attic ladder with a flashlight (check the batteries) will tell you without having to climb all the way in there."
- Small asides in parentheses are fine when they help the reader.

## Cite sources to back a recommendation, not to decorate an observation
- Kept: UMN Extension on ceiling air leaks causing ice dams; "The University of Minnesota and MN Department of Commerce both emphasize fixing air leaks first."
- Cut: naming an inspector for where frost forms first; quoting the Home Energy Guide to restate a process already explained.
- Rule: name the source when the reader is being told what to do or why to believe a surprising claim. Otherwise explain it plainly and keep the source in the list at the end.

## Softer certainty
- "is more likely from", "could end up making things worse", "Not necessarily, and likely not by itself."
- Say how likely something is, not that it always happens.

## Shorter and looser than the first draft
- Fewer lists inside sentences. One example of a leak spot beats six.
- An occasional exclamation point is fine. Keep it rare.

## Cut what does not change what the reader does (second half of the pilot)
- Cut: frost depth figures and dates, code vent ratios, the new-home blower door rule, the rebate note, and a step-by-step inspection list.
- Kept: what to fix, in order (air leaks, then insulation, then vents), and where the leaks are.
- Rule: a fact stays only if it changes what the reader checks, buys or asks a contractor for.

## Short labeled answers inside a section
- A short question on its own line, then the answer: "Where to stop leaks?" then the list. "How to stop moisture?" then three actions.
- These are plain text questions, not bold labels with colons.

## Mention the money
- "Paying for heat is pricey and it is getting pricier, air leaks mean heat leaks."
- One sentence on cost is enough. No dollar figures unless sourced.

## End by handing the reader a next step
- "You can check yourself or have a professional do it."
- Take photos to show a contractor. Check while it is still cold or during a thaw.

## Still enforced on every draft
- Physics and code claims must be checked against a source in Refs/SOURCE_LIBRARY.md.
- Spelling and grammar get cleaned up before publishing (your/you're, its/it's, peek/peak).
- No em-dashes, no bold-label bullets, 2 to 3 sentence paragraphs, FAQ of 4 questions in the page settings.
