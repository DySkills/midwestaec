Balloon Framing: Why Older Midwest Two-Stories Are Drafty and How to Tell If Yours Is One

In a balloon-framed house the wall cavities run from the basement to the attic with nothing in between. That makes the house drafty in winter and lets a fire climb inside the walls.

By Midwest AEC Building Science • Published November 2026 • NIOSH F2002-49 / BSC BSD-109 / NPS Preservation Brief 3
Quick Summary

Many Midwest two-stories built before about 1940 are balloon framed. The outside wall studs run in one piece from the foundation to the roof, so the space between them is open from the basement to the attic. In winter that space works like a chimney, and in a fire it lets flames climb inside the walls. You can check for it in five minutes from the basement, and the fix doesn't require opening the walls.

  • Look up between the floor joists from the basement. If you can see into the wall, it's balloon framed.
  • The drafts come from air moving up the walls, not through the windows.
  • The fix is blocking the bottom and top of each stud bay, then insulating.

If your second floor is cold along the outside walls, the baseboards feel drafty and the basement sill area pulls in cold air, the house may be balloon framed. It was the common way to build two-story houses in Midwest cities and rail towns from the late 1800s until about 1940, and plenty of them are still standing in Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Louis, Minneapolis and Detroit.

What Makes Balloon Framing Different?

In a modern house each floor is built as a platform, and the walls of the next floor sit on top of it. That platform closes off the wall cavities at every floor.

In a balloon-framed house the outside studs run the full height of the house, often 20 feet or more. The second-floor joists rest on a board, called a ribbon, notched into the inside of the studs. Nothing closes the wall at the floor line, so every stud bay is an open shaft about 3.5 by 14.5 inches, from the basement to the attic.

Why Is It So Drafty?

Warm air rises. In winter the warm air inside your house pushes up and out at the top, and cold air gets pulled in down low to replace it. Building Science Corporation calls this the stack effect (BSD-109, Pressures in Buildings), and it gets stronger the colder it is outside.

In a balloon-framed house, every open stud bay gives that air a straight path. Cold air comes in at the basement sill, moves up inside the walls past the plaster, and warm air escapes into the attic at the top. That's why the floors along the outside walls feel cold and the second floor never seems to hold heat, whether you heat with gas, electricity, propane or wood.

Why Is It a Fire Risk?

The same open path that moves air in winter moves fire and smoke. A NIOSH firefighter fatality report (F2002-49) describes the balloon-frame void as allowing "hidden fire and smoke that penetrate the wall space to spread vertically for two or three floors." Because the fire travels inside the walls, it can reach the attic before anyone in the rooms sees it.

Blocking each bay at the bottom and the top doesn't make the house fireproof, but it takes away the open chimney.

How Do I Check My House?

Does the basement show open bays?

Go to the basement and shine a flashlight straight up between the floor joists where they meet an outside wall. If you can see up into the wall cavity, the house is balloon framed.

Does the attic show open bays?

In the attic, look along the eaves where the outside walls end. Open, dark stud bays dropping down into the walls mean nothing is blocking the top.

Are there interior walls like this too?

Many older houses also have a balloon-framed bearing wall down the center hallway. Check the basement under it the same way.

What Does the Fix Involve?

You don't need to tear out plaster. Air needs a way in and a way out to keep moving, so blocking the two open ends stops it: the bottom of each bay at the basement sill, and the top of each bay in the attic or behind the knee walls.

Each bay gets filled with mineral wool or solid wood blocking, and the edges are sealed with fire-rated sealant. Once both ends are closed, the walls can be dense-packed with cellulose from the outside. Building Science Corporation measured about a tenfold drop in air leakage through test walls dense-packed to around 3.5 pounds per cubic foot (Lstiburek, BSI-043).

The National Park Service's guidance for historic buildings (Preservation Brief 3, Hensley and Aguilar, 2011) calls reducing air leakage "the first priority" of a preservation retrofit. It puts attic and basement work next and wall insulation after that, which keeps the original plaster in place.

Things to Know Before You Hire Someone

Check for knob-and-tube wiring before any insulation goes in, since it shouldn't be buried in insulation while it is live. Ask for a blower door test before and after, so you can see how much the leakage dropped. And if the house is brick, read up on how insulating solid brick walls changes how they dry.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is balloon framing?

It is a way of building where the outside wall studs run in one piece from the foundation to the roof, and the upper floors hang off the side of them on a board called a ribbon. Because no plate closes the wall at each floor, the space between the studs is open from the basement to the attic.

How do I know if my house is balloon framed?

From the basement, shine a flashlight up between the floor joists along an outside wall. If you can see up into the wall cavity, it is balloon framed. In the attic you may also see open stud bays dropping down into the walls at the eaves.

Why is a balloon-framed house so drafty?

Warm air rises up the open wall cavities and escapes into the attic, and cold air gets pulled in at the bottom to replace it. Each stud bay works like a small chimney, so the second floor loses heat and the floors near the outside walls feel cold.

Is balloon framing a fire risk?

Yes. A NIOSH firefighter fatality report describes the open balloon-frame cavity as letting hidden fire and smoke spread vertically for two or three floors. Blocking the bay at the bottom and the top slows that spread.
