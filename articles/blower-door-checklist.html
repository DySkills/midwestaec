How to Pass a Blower Door Test on a Midwest Remodel: The Leaks to Seal First

A blower door test shows how leaky a house is and where the air gets out. Here is what the numbers mean and the leaks that make the biggest difference, in the order to tackle them.

By Midwest AEC Building Science • Published November 2026 • LBNL Air Leakage Database / BSC BSD-104 / BSC BSD-135
Quick Summary

A blower door test puts a number on how leaky a house is and lets a tester find the leaks while you watch. On a remodel, the best time to use it is while the walls are open, so you can fix what it finds. The biggest leaks in Midwest homes are almost always in the same places: the rim joist, the attic floor, chases and ductwork. Seal those first.

  • Test before work starts and again before drywall.
  • Big holes beat small cracks. Seal chases, soffits and the attic hatch first.
  • The rim joist and sill plate are usually the biggest basement leak.
  • Weatherization typically cuts leakage about 35 percent; open walls let you do more.

Whether your city requires a blower door test or you just want the remodel to make the house more comfortable, the test is the best way to know if the air sealing worked. Requirements vary a lot across the Midwest.

Some states and cities require new homes to pass a test, others don't, and remodels often aren't required to test at all. Check with your local building department.

What Does the Test Measure?

A technician mounts a large fan in an exterior doorway and pulls air out of the house until the inside sits 50 pascals below outdoors, about the pressure of a 20 mph wind on every surface. The amount of air the fan moves to hold that pressure is the leakage, reported as CFM50 (cubic feet per minute) or ACH50 (how many times the house's air volume would change per hour at that pressure).

While the fan runs, every leak in the house is pulling air in. A tester with a smoke pencil or an infrared camera can walk around and find them.

How Much Better Can It Get?

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's US air leakage database gives a sense of what to expect (Chan and Sherman, 2011). Weatherization programs cut the median home's leakage by about 35 percent, and other energy retrofits by about 22 percent. New homes built after 2006 had a median leakage about a third of the weatherization homes before their work.

On a remodel with walls and ceilings open, you can reach leaks that weatherization crews can't, so you can often beat those numbers.

The Ten Leaks to Seal First

Building Science Corporation's air barrier digest (Lstiburek, BSD-104) treats air sealing as making one continuous air barrier around the house. These are the places that barrier is usually broken, roughly in order of how much they leak.

1
The attic hatch or pull-down stairs is one big unsealed hole in the ceiling. Weatherstrip it, insulate the lid and add a latch that pulls it tight.
2
Plumbing and flue chases are open shafts from the basement to the attic. Cap them at the top with rigid board, and use sheet metal and fire-rated sealant around flues.
3
Dropped soffits over kitchen cabinets and tubs are often open to the attic. Cover them from above.
4
The rim joist and sill plate, where the house framing sits on the foundation, leak through every seam. Seal and insulate them with rigid foam cut to fit and sealed at the edges, or with spray foam.
5
Ductwork in attics, crawl spaces and garages pushes and pulls air through the house when it leaks. Seal joints with mastic, not cloth tape. Building Science Corporation's ice dam digest (BSD-135) also warns against ducts in attics.
6
Older recessed lights are open to the attic. Replace them with airtight-rated fixtures or cover them with fire-rated boxes.
7
The top plates of interior walls leave a gap where the drywall meets the plate, plus wire and pipe holes. Seal them from the attic side.
8
Bath and kitchen fans leak around the housing. Seal the housing to the drywall, and make sure the duct runs outside with a working damper.
9
Windows and doors leave a gap between the frame and the rough opening. Seal it with low-expansion foam or backer rod and sealant before the trim goes on.
10
Outlets, switches and penetrations in exterior walls leak too. Use foam gaskets behind cover plates, and seal wire and pipe holes through the wall.

When to Test

Before work starts?

A test before demolition shows the baseline and the biggest leaks. It's also a good check on old balloon framing or open chases that you'll want to block while the walls are open.

→ How to Firestop and Air-Seal Balloon Framing Without Tearing Out Plaster

Before drywall?

The most useful test is after air sealing but before insulation and drywall cover it up. Any leak the tester finds is still easy to fix.

At the end?

A final test gives you the number, and if your city requires one, it's the one that counts.

Seal Tight, Then Ventilate

The tighter the house gets, the more it needs planned fresh air from a fan or a ventilator. Plan the ventilation as part of the remodel.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a blower door test measure?

A fan mounted in an outside door pulls air out of the house until the inside is at a set pressure, usually 50 pascals below outdoors. The airflow needed to hold that pressure shows how leaky the house is, often reported as air changes per hour at 50 pascals (ACH50) or cubic feet per minute at 50 pascals (CFM50).

Is a blower door test required?

It depends on where you live. Many Midwest cities and states require new homes to pass a blower door test, but requirements vary by state and city, and remodels often aren't required to be tested. Check with your local building department.

How much can air sealing reduce leakage?

In Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory data, weatherization programs cut median home air leakage by about 35 percent, and other energy efficiency retrofits by about 22 percent. A remodel with the walls open can often do better, because the leaks are easier to reach.

When should a blower door test be done on a remodel?

Twice if you can: once before work starts, to find the big leaks, and once after air sealing but before drywall or insulation covers it, so any remaining leaks can still be fixed. A final test at the end shows the result.
