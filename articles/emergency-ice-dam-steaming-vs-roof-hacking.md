# Ice Dam Leaking Right Now? Steaming vs.

Chipping, and What to Do Today

> **Quick Summary:** If water is dripping from the ceiling, the ice is not the leak, it's the dam holding back water that backs up under the shingles. Today, get the snow off the lower roof from the ground and give the water a way to drain. Don't chip the ice. If it has to come off, low-pressure steam is the roof-safe way to do it. Then fix the attic air leaks that made the ice, or it will be back next storm.
>
> - Rake snow off the bottom few feet of the roof from the ground.
> - Don't chip, hammer or pressure-wash the ice.
> - Hire low-pressure steam if the ice has to come off.
> - Seal attic air leaks so it doesn't happen again.

---

If you are here because there's a stain spreading across the ceiling or water running down the inside of a wall, you already know what the problem is. Here is what to do about it today, and what to do once the weather lets up.

## Why Is Water Coming In?

Snow on the upper roof melts because the roof deck is warm, warmed by heat leaking up from the house. The water runs down to the eaves, which hang out past the warm walls and stay cold, and it freezes there. That ridge of ice is the dam.

More meltwater keeps coming down behind it and pools. Shingles shed water running downhill, but they aren't built to hold back standing water, so it seeps up under them and into the house.

## What Can I Do Today?

Get the snow off the bottom of the roof.
A roof rake used from the ground pulls snow off the lower few feet of the roof, so there is less to melt and feed the dam. The University of Minnesota notes a rake can damage roofing, so pull gently and leave a thin layer rather than scraping down to the shingles.

Give the water a way out.
A channel through the dam lets the pooled water drain. Some people fill a stocking with calcium chloride and lay it across the ice so it melts a path.

It works, but the University of Minnesota says a channel stops working within days and is only a temporary fix.

Catch what's coming in.
Put buckets and towels down, and poke a small hole in a sagging, water-filled ceiling so it drains into a bucket instead of coming down all at once. Take photos for your insurance company.

## Steam or Chipping?

Ice grips shingles hard. Swinging a hammer, ice pick or axe at it tends to break shingles, tear the seal strips, and dent the roof deck, which can leave you with a real roof leak after the ice is gone. It's also a quick way to fall off a ladder.

What do pros use?
Most ice dam companies use a low-pressure steamer. It melts the ice with heat and low pressure, so it cuts through without blasting the shingles.

A high-pressure washer, even with hot water, can strip the granules off the shingles and drive water under them.

What should I ask?
Ask what pressure the machine runs at, and whether they carry insurance for roof work. If someone shows up with a pressure washer or a hatchet, keep looking.

## Why Did This Happen?

Ice dams are a heat leak problem that shows up on the roof. Building Science Corporation's ice dam digest (Straube, BSD-135) explains that the fix is keeping the roof deck cold enough that the snow doesn't melt in the first place.

Where is the heat coming from?
The University of Minnesota says air leaking into the attic is the main way heat gets there in many homes. Common spots are recessed lights, the attic hatch, plumbing and wiring holes, and ducts running through the attic.

Knee-wall spaces in 1.5-story homes leak the same way.

## What Fixes It for Good?

Fix things in this order. Seal the air leaks into the attic first, since that's the biggest heat source. Then add insulation over the sealed ceiling, then make sure the attic is vented from the eaves to the ridge so the deck stays cold.

&rarr; [Attic Air Sealing and Baffles: A Step-by-Step Guide](attic-air-sealing-baffles.html)

Heating costs are only going up, and the heat melting your roof is heat you already paid for.

When the roof is clear and dry, a quick climb up the attic ladder with a flashlight will show you a lot: dark stains on the decking, frost on nail tips, or bare spots in the insulation over the leaky areas. You can check yourself, or have an energy auditor or insulation contractor do it.
