# High-Efficiency Furnace Shut Off in a Blizzard? Check the Vent Pipes Outside First

> **Quick Summary:** A high-efficiency furnace pulls combustion air in through one plastic pipe and pushes exhaust out another, usually on a side wall. When snow drifts over the pipe ends, or frost and ice build up inside, the furnace's pressure switch senses the blockage and shuts it down for safety. Most of the time the fix is clearing the pipes. Placing them a foot above your typical snow depth keeps it from happening again.
>
> - Two white or gray plastic pipes on the side of the house mean a condensing furnace.
> - Clear snow gently by hand. Never use a flame or chip the pipe.
> - Keep vent ends a foot above your usual maximum snow depth.
> - Keep carbon monoxide alarms working on every level.

---

It's 6 a.m. after a night of blowing snow, and the house is getting cold. The furnace starts, runs a few seconds and shuts off, over and over.

Before you call for emergency service, walk outside to where two plastic pipes come out of the wall. If they're buried in a drift, you've probably found it.

## Why Does This Happen?

High-efficiency gas and propane furnaces, the kind most Midwest homes have installed in recent decades, cool their exhaust so much that water condenses out of it. That's where the extra efficiency comes from. The cool, wet exhaust can't go up an old chimney, so it goes out a plastic pipe, and the furnace often pulls its combustion air in through a second pipe next to it.

Is snow covering the pipes?
Drifting snow can cover both pipe ends, especially on the windward side of the house or under a roof that sheds snow.

Is ice building up inside?
Exhaust is warm and full of moisture. In extreme cold, that moisture can freeze at the pipe end or in a long run of exposed pipe, gradually narrowing it until the furnace can't push enough exhaust out.

What does the furnace do then?
It uses a pressure switch to confirm the vent is clear before it lights. When the vent is blocked, the switch doesn't close, and the furnace won't fire, or it lights and then shuts off. Many furnaces lock out after several failed tries.

## How Do I Check and Clear It Safely?

Turn the furnace off at the thermostat first. Then go outside and gently clear snow away from both pipe ends with your hands or a brush. Look into each pipe with a flashlight for ice.

Should I chip out the ice?
No. Don't hit the pipe or poke it with a tool, since cracked PVC can leak exhaust. Don't use an open flame or a heat gun. A hair dryer on low, from a safe distance, can soften a thin layer at the opening.

Then what?
Turn the furnace back on. If it lights and keeps running, keep an eye on the pipes through the storm. If there's ice deep in the pipe, the furnace still won't run, or it shows a lockout code, call an HVAC technician.

Is there a safety concern?
Yes. A blocked exhaust can push combustion gases, including carbon monoxide, back toward the house. Keep carbon monoxide alarms working on every level and near bedrooms, and don't keep restarting a furnace with a vent you know is blocked.

## How Do I Keep It From Happening Again?

Are the pipes high enough?
A DOE Building America guideline for high-efficiency gas furnaces (Brand and Rose, 2012) recommends locating the vent terminal "one foot above the maximum snow level for that climate." In heavy snow areas, like the Lake Superior and Lake Michigan snowbelts, that often means a raised termination kit or a roof termination.

Does the pipe slope back to the furnace?
The same guideline says vent piping should slope back toward the furnace and its condensate drain at a quarter inch per foot, and calls this "very important in cold climates." Water that drains back can't sit in the pipe and freeze.

How much pipe is outside?
The guideline also limits how much pipe is exposed to outdoor air, to avoid freeze-up. Long runs on the outside of the wall are a common cause of repeated icing.

Is the intake too close to the exhaust?
The guideline warns against putting the combustion air intake above the exhaust, where it can pull exhaust back in. Follow the furnace maker's spacing.

If your vents freeze every cold snap, ask a technician to check the slope, the length of exposed pipe and the termination height against the furnace's installation manual.
