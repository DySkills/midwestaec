Insulating Firebrick vs. Dense Firebrick: Which One for a Fireplace, Wood Stove, Pizza Oven or Forge

They look alike and both handle fire, but one is built to hold heat and take a beating, and the other is built to keep heat in and crumbles if you hit it. Here is how to pick.

By Midwest AEC Building Science • Published November 2026 • ASTM C155 / ASTM C1261 / manufacturer data
Quick Summary

"Firebrick" covers two very different bricks. Dense firebrick is heavy and strong and soaks up heat, which is what you want lining a fireplace or a wood stove. Insulating firebrick is light enough to carve with a saw and keeps heat in, which is what you want in a kiln or a forge wall. Using the wrong one either wastes heat or crumbles in a season.

  • Dense firebrick weighs about 133 pounds per cubic foot. Insulating firebrick weighs about 33 to 50.
  • Insulating firebrick conducts heat roughly five to seven times less than dense firebrick.
  • Dense firebrick is more than twenty times stronger, so it handles logs, tools and abrasion.
  • Match the insulating brick's group number to the temperature it will see.

If you searched "insulating brick," you probably have a project with fire in it: relining a wood stove, building a pizza oven in the backyard, or putting together a small forge. Across the rural Midwest, where plenty of homes heat with wood, a cracked stove lining is one of the most common reasons people go looking. The two kinds of firebrick look alike on the shelf, but they do opposite jobs.

What Is Dense Firebrick?

Dense firebrick is the heavy, tan or yellow brick you see lining fireplaces and wood stoves. It's made from fireclay and fired hard. One manufacturer's high-duty firebrick weighs 133.7 pounds per cubic foot (Alsey Refractories, Jet D.P. data sheet), about as heavy as a regular building brick.

Its job is to take direct flame and physical abuse and to store heat. It's strong, with crushing strengths in the thousands of pounds per square inch on manufacturer sheets. Because it's dense, it soaks up a lot of heat and gives it back slowly, which is why a fireplace or a masonry oven keeps radiating long after the fire dies down.

What Is Insulating Firebrick?

Insulating firebrick, often sold as IFB, is so light it almost feels like foam. Morgan Thermal Ceramics lists its TJM 23 brick at 33.1 pounds per cubic foot and its TJM 26 at 49.9. It's full of tiny air pockets, which is what makes it insulate.

Its job is to keep heat where it is. It heats up fast, stores very little, and slows the heat trying to get through it. You can cut and carve it with a hand saw, which makes it popular for kilns, forges and small foundries.

How Different Are They, in Numbers?

Here is how two manufacturers' data sheets compare:

Property Dense firebrick (Alsey Jet D.P.) Insulating firebrick (Morgan TJM 23)
Weight 133.7 lb per cubic foot 33.1 lb per cubic foot
Heat flow at about 400°C (750°F) 1.23 W/m·K 0.18 W/m·K
Heat flow at about 800°C (1,470°F) about 1.3 W/m·K 0.25 W/m·K

The insulating brick lets through roughly five to seven times less heat, depending on temperature. Strength goes the other way: Morgan lists TJM 23 at 145 psi crushing strength, while medium-duty fireclay brick from NEUEX is listed at 3,500 to 4,500 psi, more than twenty times stronger. Values vary by product, so check the sheet for the exact brick you buy.

What Do the Group Numbers Mean?

Insulating firebrick is graded by ASTM C155 into groups like 16, 20, 23, 26 and 28. The number roughly tracks the temperature it's rated for: Group 20 is tested at 1,950°F, Group 23 at 2,250°F and Group 26 at 2,550°F. To pass, the brick can't shrink or grow more than 2 percent after being reheated at that temperature.

Higher groups handle more heat but are heavier and insulate a little less. Pick the lowest group comfortably above the hottest temperature your project will reach.

Which One Goes Where?

Fireplace firebox?

Dense brick. ASTM C1261, the standard for residential firebox brick, calls for brick that is 100 percent solid with no cores, with a minimum strength, set with fireclay or refractory mortar in joints about an eighth of an inch thick. Insulating brick is too soft to stand up to logs and a fireplace poker.

Ordinary red building brick isn't made for direct fire, so don't use it to patch a firebox either.

Wood stove lining?

Replace it with what the stove maker specifies, usually dense firebrick cut to the same size. The bricks protect the steel and store heat, and many stove makers also list the exact brick in the manual. Cracked bricks can stay in place if they're still whole, but replace any that are crumbling or missing.

Pizza oven?

Usually both. Dense brick for the cooking floor and the dome, because the floor has to take a peel scraping across it and the dome has to store heat for baking. Insulating brick or other insulation outside and under that, to keep the stored heat from leaking away into the stand.

Forge, kiln or foundry?

Insulating brick for the walls, where it keeps the heat in and lets the space heat up quickly. Electric kilns are mostly built from it. In a forge, protect the floor where hot steel and flux land, since insulating brick wears away under abrasion and chemical attack.

Is the Dust Safe?

Cutting either kind makes dust. Morgan's safety data sheet for its insulating refractory brick warns that the dust "may cause cancer by inhalation," because respirable dust may contain crystalline silica. It recommends hand tools "whenever possible," dust collection when using power tools, and HEPA vacuuming or wet sweeping instead of compressed air.

Cut outside, wet the brick or the cut line when you can, and wear a NIOSH-approved particulate respirator, eye protection and gloves.

When to Call Someone

If the firebox of a fireplace is cracked through, the mortar joints are open, or you see gaps where the firebox meets the smoke chamber, have a chimney professional look at it before the next fire. Those are paths for heat and sparks into the framing around the fireplace.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between insulating firebrick and regular firebrick?

Dense firebrick is heavy, around 133 pounds per cubic foot, strong, and stores heat. Insulating firebrick is very light, about 33 to 50 pounds per cubic foot, and conducts heat roughly five to seven times less, so it keeps heat in instead of soaking it up. It is also far weaker and wears away under abrasion.

Can I use insulating firebrick in my fireplace?

Not for the firebox lining. Fireplace fireboxes are lined with solid, dense firebox brick, which ASTM C1261 describes as 100 percent solid with a minimum strength. Insulating brick is too soft to stand up to logs and fireplace tools.

What do the numbers on insulating firebrick mean?

The number is the brick's group under ASTM C155 and roughly tracks its temperature rating. A Group 23 brick is tested at 2,250 degrees F and a Group 26 at 2,550 degrees F. Pick a group rated above the hottest temperature the brick will see.

Is cutting firebrick dangerous?

The dust can be. A manufacturer's safety data sheet for insulating brick warns the dust may contain crystalline silica and may cause cancer by inhalation. Cut with hand tools where you can, keep the dust down with water or a HEPA vacuum, and wear a particulate respirator.
