Why Are My Windows Wet Inside in Winter? Indoor Humidity, Cold Glass and What to Fix

Water running down the inside of the glass on a cold morning means the air in the house is holding more moisture than the glass can handle. Here is the humidity to aim for as it gets colder, and when the window is the problem.

By Midwest AEC Building Science • Published November 2026 • MN CEE humidity guidance / UMN Extension / MU Extension EM112
Quick Summary

Condensation on the inside of a window means warm, moist indoor air is touching glass colder than its dew point, like a cold drink sweating on a summer day. The colder it gets outside, the colder the glass, and the less moisture the air can hold before it shows up on the window. The fix is usually lower indoor humidity first, then warmer glass if needed.

  • Turn indoor humidity down as outdoor temperatures drop.
  • Run bathroom and kitchen fans while you use them and for a while after.
  • Open curtains and blinds so warm room air reaches the glass.
  • If windows still sweat at low humidity, the glass is the problem.

If you wake up to water pooled on the window sills or frost on the inside of the glass, your house is telling you something about the air inside it. Here is what to check before you start pricing new windows.

Why Does This Happen?

Air holds water vapor, and warm air holds more than cold air. When room air touches something cold, it cools down, and if it cools past its dew point the extra water comes out as liquid. A glass of ice water does the same thing on a humid day.

In winter, the coldest surface in most rooms is the window glass. The colder it is outside, the colder the glass, and the less humidity it takes to fog it.

What Humidity Should I Aim For?

Lower than you might think, and lower as it gets colder. Minnesota's Center for Energy and Environment gives these targets:

Outdoor temperature Keep indoor humidity
20 to 40°F below 40%
10 to 20°F below 35%
0 to 10°F below 30%
-10 to 0°F below 25%
-20 to -10°F below 20%
below -20°F below 15%

A cheap digital humidity gauge will tell you where you are. If you run a humidifier and see condensation, CEE says to lower the setting.

How Do I Bring the Humidity Down?

Use the fans you have.

Showers, cooking and drying laundry put a lot of water in the air. Run the bath fan during and after showers and the range hood while cooking.

Make sure they vent outside, not into the attic.

Turn down or turn off the humidifier.

A furnace or whole-house humidifier set for comfort in November can be far too high in January. Many homes in tight newer construction don't need one at all.

Look for hidden sources.

A wet basement or crawlspace, lots of houseplants, firewood stored indoors, or an unvented dryer can all add moisture. The University of Minnesota notes that above 50 percent humidity, moisture problems are likely.

Help the Glass Stay Warm

Open curtains and blinds during the day, and leave a gap at night. Heavy drapes trap the glass behind them away from warm room air, so it gets colder and sweats more. Keep heating vents and baseboards under windows uncovered for the same reason.

When Is It the Window?

If a window still sweats when the humidity is in range, the glass is too cold. Single-pane glass, old aluminum frames, and double-pane units with a failed seal (fog between the panes) are the usual cases.

What are my options?

Interior or exterior storm windows add a layer and warm the inner glass, often for much less than replacement. Newer windows with better glass and warm-edge spacers run warmer at the edges, where condensation usually starts.

Check your humidity on the next cold morning and write down the number and the outdoor temperature. That one reading will tell you whether to start with the air or the windows, and it's useful to show a contractor too.

Frequently Asked Questions

What indoor humidity should I keep in winter to stop window condensation?

Lower as it gets colder. Minnesota's Center for Energy and Environment suggests below 40 percent when it's 20 to 40°F outside, below 30 percent at 0 to 10°F, and below 20 percent at -10 to -20°F.

Is condensation on the inside of windows bad?

A little fog at the bottom of the glass on the coldest mornings is common. Water that runs down onto the sill, or frost that builds up day after day, can rot wood sills, peel paint and grow mold, so it's worth fixing.

Why do only some of my windows sweat?

Usually because those rooms are more humid, like bathrooms, kitchens and bedrooms with the door closed, or because those windows are colder. Single-pane glass, failed double-pane seals, and windows behind heavy curtains run colder.

Do I need new windows to stop condensation?

Not necessarily. Start by lowering indoor humidity and running bath and kitchen fans. If windows still sweat at reasonable humidity, better windows or interior storm windows that keep the inner glass warmer can help.
