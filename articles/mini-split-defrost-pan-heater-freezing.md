# Mini-Split Frozen or Steaming Outside? Defrost Cycles, Snow and Ice in a Midwest Winter

> **Quick Summary:** In winter, the outdoor unit of a heat pump pulls heat out of cold outdoor air, and its coil runs colder than the air around it. Moisture in the air freezes on the coil, the same way frost forms on a cold car window. Every so often the unit stops heating, runs a defrost cycle to melt it, and you see steam. That's normal. What isn't normal is ice that won't clear, a unit buried in snow, or meltwater freezing into a block underneath it.
>
> - Frost on the coil and steam during defrost are normal.
> - A unit encased in ice or buried in snow needs attention.
> - The unit should sit above your usual snow depth, out from under roof drips.
> - Meltwater needs a place to drain without refreezing on the unit.

---

If you just got a heat pump or mini-split and you look out on a cold, damp morning to see the outdoor unit white with frost or blowing steam, it can look like something's wrong. Most of the time it's doing exactly what it should.

## Why Does It Frost Up?

To heat your house, the outdoor coil has to be colder than the outdoor air, so heat flows into it. When the air is near freezing and damp, the moisture in it condenses and freezes on that cold coil. Frost tends to build fastest on damp days near freezing, which describes a lot of Midwest winter days.

What does defrost look like?
When frost starts blocking the coil, the unit reverses for a few minutes to warm the coil and melt it off. You may see steam outside, hear a whoosh, and the indoor head may stop blowing warm air for a bit.

Then it goes back to heating.

## When Is It a Problem?

Ice that doesn't clear.
If the coil stays coated in solid ice for hours, or the ice keeps getting thicker after defrost cycles, call your installer. It can mean a defrost control, sensor or refrigerant problem.

Ice built up under the unit.
Defrost makes water. If the unit sits too low, that water can freeze into a mound under it, block the drain holes in the base, and eventually build up into the coil and fan.

Snow or roof water.
A unit buried in a drift can't move air. A unit under a roof edge with no gutter gets dripped on, and the water freezes on the coil.

## Where Should the Unit Sit?

The cold-climate installation guide from the Northeast Energy Efficiency Partnerships (NEEP) covers exactly this.

How high?
NEEP's guide says to put the unit above your area's typical maximum snow depth, on a factory-approved stand or wall bracket. Ground-mounted stands need well-drained soil that won't heave with frost.

What about the roof?
The guide says not to install the unit directly under a roof drip line. If there's no way around it and no gutter, use a manufacturer-approved drip cap or shield.

Where does the water go?
Keep the unit away from walkways and steps, since defrost meltwater that refreezes is a slip hazard.

## Do I Need a Base Pan Heater?

Some units come with a small electric heater in the base pan to keep the drain holes from freezing shut. NEEP's guide says drain pan heaters are generally not needed when the unit has good ground clearance and is protected from dripping water.

They earn their keep where meltwater can't drain away. Ask your installer whether yours has one and whether it's on.

## What Can I Do?

Keep the area around the unit clear of snow after storms, and brush snow off the top. Don't chip ice off the coil, since the fins bend easily. If you have to clear ice, let the unit defrost or pour lukewarm water over it.
