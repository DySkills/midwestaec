# Rammed Earth in the Midwest: Thermal Mass, Freeze-Thaw and What Research Says It Takes

> **Quick Summary:** Rammed earth walls are made by compacting damp soil into forms in layers until it's dense and strong. They're common in dry climates. In the Midwest, three things decide whether they last: insulation, because earth stores heat but doesn't stop it; stabilization, usually with cement; and keeping water off the walls. A cold-climate project published by HUD shows it can be done.
>
> - Thermal mass is not insulation. Plan for real insulation in the wall.
> - Stabilized rammed earth can be nearly as strong as low-strength concrete.
> - Fibers improved freeze-thaw durability in cold-climate testing.
> - Wide overhangs, a raised base and good drainage protect the walls.

---

Rammed earth gets attention for its look and its low processing, and some people want it in Midwest homes, either as walls or as earthen floors. The questions are always the same: will it keep the house warm, and will it survive the freeze-thaw cycles?

## Mass Is Not Insulation

A thick earth wall stores a lot of heat and releases it slowly, which evens out day-to-night swings. That's thermal mass. It's different from insulation, which slows heat flowing through the wall.

A solid earth wall in a Midwest winter still loses heat steadily to the outside, it just does it slowly.

The cold-climate project described by Gangarao, Johnson, Liang and Blanford in HUD's *Cityscape* journal (2020) handled this by building the wall as a sandwich: two 8-inch layers of stabilized rammed earth with 8 inches of foam insulation between them, for about R-40. With that insulation and good airtightness, the authors report the home's yearly space heating load was roughly 75 percent lower than average homes in the area.

The same research also notes the downside of mass: a heavy house is slow to warm up again after the heat has been turned down.

## Does It Survive Freezing?

Wet earth that freezes can crumble, so moisture is the risk. The researchers ran stabilized rammed earth samples through freeze-thaw cycles and then tested their strength against samples that hadn't been frozen. Adding fibers improved both bending strength and freeze-thaw durability, and the authors call the long-term freeze-thaw outlook for their cold-climate building "very promising."

How strong is it?
The research found stabilized rammed earth "nearly as strong as low-strength (~2500 psi) concrete." Portland cement is the most common stabilizer for modern rammed earth.

How do I keep water off it?
Build on a raised, water-resistant base so splashback and snow don't sit against the earth. Give the roof generous overhangs. Grade the ground away from the walls, and keep downspouts away from the base.

## What About Earthen Floors?

Earthen floors are poured or tamped indoors over a prepared base and sealed with oil. In a Midwest basement or slab-on-grade, they face the same issue any floor does: moisture coming up from the ground. Put a capillary break of crushed stone and a vapor barrier under the floor, add rigid insulation for a warmer floor, and keep the room's summer humidity down.

## Testing Your Soil

Is my soil right for it?
A simple jar test, shaking subsoil in a jar of water and letting it settle, shows roughly how much sand, silt and clay it has. Use soil from below the topsoil, never the organic topsoil itself.

What does a builder need?
For design, a lab grain-size analysis by sieve and hydrometer (ASTM D6913 and D7928, which replaced the older D422) gives the gradation an engineer or experienced builder needs to choose a mix and stabilizer.

## Get the Right People

Rammed earth isn't covered in detail by most Midwest building departments, so expect to work with an engineer and a builder who has done it before, and to explain the design during permitting.
