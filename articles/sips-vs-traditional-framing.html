SIPs vs. Stick Framing in the Midwest: What You Gain, What It Costs, and the Joint Detail That Matters Most

Structural insulated panels put a solid foam core between two skins, so there are far fewer studs to leak heat. They can be very airtight, but only if every joint is sealed, as cold-climate roof failures in Alaska showed.

By Midwest AEC Building Science • Published November 2026 • BSC BSI-036 / BSC BSD-163
Quick Summary

A structural insulated panel (SIP) is a thick foam core glued between two sheets of OSB. The foam runs continuously across the wall, so there are far fewer studs bridging heat out, and big panels mean fewer seams to leak. Those gains depend on one detail: every joint between panels has to be airtight. Building Science Corporation documented rotting SIP roofs in Juneau, Alaska where it wasn't.

  • Continuous foam means much less heat bridging than stud walls.
  • Large panels go up fast and have fewer seams.
  • Seal every panel joint, especially at the roof ridge and over beams.
  • Compare finished shell costs, not panels against lumber.

If you're building a new home in Minnesota or the Dakotas and the builder offers SIPs, the pitch usually sounds great: a super-insulated, airtight shell that goes up in days. Most of that is true. The part the brochure leaves out is how much the result depends on the crew sealing the joints.

How Is a SIP Different From a Stud Wall?

In a stud wall, insulation fills the spaces between wood studs, plates and headers, and all that framing conducts heat through the wall. In a SIP, the foam core runs nearly unbroken across the whole panel, with framing only at the edges, openings and joints.

The same principle applies to stud walls with continuous foam on the outside. Building Science Corporation's condensation digest (Straube, BSD-163) explains that insulation that isn't interrupted by framing keeps the sheathing warmer and the wall drier.

The Joint Detail That Matters Most

Building Science Corporation's Joseph Lstiburek examined SIP roofs in Juneau, Alaska that were rotting along the panel joints and at the ridge. The first sign was mushrooms growing up through the shingles (BSI-036, 2010).

What caused it?

The gaps along the panel edges and splines formed tiny connected channels. Where the panel joints weren't airtight on the inside, warm, moist indoor air leaked into them, rose toward the ridge by the stack effect, and condensed on the cold outer skin. Lstiburek called the pattern "ridge rot."

How is it prevented?

His recommendations: "Make the panel joints airtight, especially over structural supports such as beams and purlins," and provide a way to remove moisture, such as a vented over-roof. Follow the panel maker's sealing details for every joint, and inspect them before the interior finish covers them.

Juneau's climate is wet and cold for long stretches, but the physics is the same in a Minnesota or Michigan winter: moist air rising into a cold roof will condense where it finds a path.

Is a SIP House Airtight?

It can be very airtight, because there are fewer seams. But a SIP house is only as tight as its joints, window openings, and roof-to-wall connections. Ask the builder for a blower door test target in the contract, with a test before the interior finish goes on.

A tight house also needs planned ventilation, usually an HRV or ERV.

What About Cost?

The panels usually cost more than lumber and batts, but they replace several steps at once: framing, sheathing, insulation and part of the air sealing. They also go up quickly, which can shorten the schedule. Get bids for the finished, insulated, air-sealed shell from both a SIP builder and a stick builder, and compare those, not panels against a lumber package.

Things to Ask a SIP Builder

How are the joints sealed, and who inspects them? How are electrical runs handled, since you can't drill freely through the foam?

How is the roof vented or protected at the ridge? What blower door number will the house meet?

Frequently Asked Questions

Are SIPs better than stick framing?

They can perform better because the foam core is continuous, with far fewer studs bridging heat through the wall, and the large panels have fewer seams. That advantage depends on careful joint sealing. A well-built stick-framed wall with exterior insulation and good air sealing can perform similarly.

What went wrong with SIP roofs in Juneau, Alaska?

Building Science Corporation's Joseph Lstiburek examined SIP roofs there with rot along the panel joints and the ridge, which he called ridge rot. The cause was moist indoor air leaking into the panel joints and rising toward the ridge. His fix: make the panel joints airtight, especially over beams, and provide a way to remove moisture such as a vented over-roof.

Do SIPs need a vapor barrier?

The foam core itself slows vapor. What matters most is airtight joints, since air leaking into a joint carries far more moisture than diffusion. Tape or seal every joint as the panel maker specifies, and keep indoor humidity under control in winter.

Are SIPs more expensive?

The panels usually cost more than framing lumber and batts, but they go up faster and replace several steps. Compare bids for the finished, insulated, air-sealed shell, not panels versus lumber alone.
