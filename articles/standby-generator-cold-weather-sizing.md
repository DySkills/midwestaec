# Standby Generators in a Midwest Winter: Sizing, Cold Starts and Carbon Monoxide

> **Quick Summary:** When the power goes out in January, a generator keeps the heat, the well pump and the sump pump running. How big it needs to be depends mostly on how you heat. Gas and propane furnaces need little power, while electric heat needs a lot. Cold weather makes generators harder to start, which is what cold-weather kits are for. And a portable generator in the wrong spot can kill you with carbon monoxide.
>
> - Size the generator for your heating system first, then the pumps.
> - Electric heat and heat pump backup strips take far more power than a furnace blower.
> - Battery and oil heaters help a standby unit start in the cold.
> - Keep portable generators at least 20 feet from the house, and have CO alarms.

---

A winter outage is a different problem from a summer one. In July you lose the food in the fridge.

In January, you lose the heat, and in a few days you can lose the pipes too. If you're on a well, you lose water the moment the power goes.

## What Do I Need to Run?

Start with heat.
How you heat sets the size more than anything else, and Midwest homes heat a lot of different ways.

Gas or propane furnace or boiler.
The fuel makes the heat. The generator only needs to run the blower or circulator pump, the igniter and controls.

That's a modest load, and many people run it from a properly installed transfer switch.

Electric furnace, baseboard or heat pump.
Here the electricity is the heat. Electric resistance heat uses its full rating, so a 10-kilowatt heat strip draws 10,000 watts, more than many portable generators make.

A heat pump uses much less than resistance heat when it's running on its compressor, but its backup strips can switch on in deep cold. Ask your installer what your backup heat draws.

Wood stove.
A wood stove needs no power at all, which is why it's still common in rural areas. You may only need enough to run pumps, lights and the fridge.

Then add the pumps.
A well pump and sump pump are next. Pumps draw a burst of extra power when they start, so look at the starting watts on the label, not just running watts.

Add up the watts of everything you must run at once. That's the minimum. A licensed electrician can measure your actual loads and size it properly.

## Why Won't It Start When It's Cold?

Cold batteries.
A battery is a chemical reaction, and chemical reactions slow down in the cold. A generator information sheet hosted by Generac (Clifford Power, Information Sheet #32) says a fully charged battery that has all its cranking power at 80°F has only about 40 percent of it at 0°F.

Cold oil.
Oil thickens in the cold like honey in a fridge, so the engine is harder to turn over. The same sheet describes block heaters and oil heaters that keep the engine warm so it starts quickly.

What's a cold-weather kit?
For standby generators, it's usually a battery warmer and an oil or block heater, run off house power so they keep things warm until the generator is needed. In much of the upper Midwest it's worth asking for one with the install.

Keep it clear.
Snow drifted against a standby unit can block the air intake and exhaust. Clear around it after storms.

The Clifford sheet notes that where ice and snow are expected, the set should be in an enclosure built for it.

## Carbon Monoxide Comes First

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission reports that more than 80 people die every year from carbon monoxide from portable generators. Winter storms are when it happens most, because people want the generator close and out of the snow.

What does CPSC say?
Run a portable generator outside, at least 20 feet from the house, with the exhaust pointed away from the home. Never in the house or garage, even with doors and windows open, and away from doors, windows and vents.

Install CO alarms.
CPSC says to put battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms outside sleeping areas and on every level, and to test them monthly. If one goes off, get outside and call 911.

Furnace vents in deep snow can also back up exhaust. If your furnace vents through the side wall, keep that clear too.

## Do It Before the Storm

Make a list of what you'd need to run in an outage, with the watts from each label. If you have a generator already, start it once a month in winter. A licensed electrician should install the transfer switch, since plugging a generator into a wall outlet can send power back into the lines and endanger utility workers.
