Insulating Under a Basement Slab and the Rim Joist: What to Use and Why It Matters

A cold basement floor and a drafty band of framing above the foundation are two of the most common heat leaks in Midwest homes. Here is what goes under a new slab, what you can do with an existing one, and how to seal the rim joist.

By Midwest AEC Building Science • Published November 2026 • BSC BSD-103 / DOE Building America
Quick Summary

The ground under a basement stays cool all year, so an uninsulated slab pulls heat out of the room above it and stays cold enough for summer air to condense on it. The rim joist, the band of framing that sits on top of the foundation, is cold and leaky in every Midwest house. Rigid foam under a new slab and foam at the rim fix both. An existing slab can be insulated on top.

  • Under a new slab: crushed stone, rigid foam, then plastic right against the concrete.
  • No sand between the plastic and the slab.
  • Insulate the slab edge, where heat leaves fastest.
  • Seal rim joists with rigid foam or spray foam, not fiberglass.

If you're pouring a new basement floor, building an addition, or finishing a basement and wondering why the floor and the top of the walls feel so cold, these two spots are where to look.

Why the Slab Loses Heat

Concrete conducts heat well, and the soil under a basement sits near its year-round temperature. Heat from the room above flows down through the slab and out through its edges into the cooler ground and the cold foundation wall. Building Science Corporation's basement guide (Lstiburek, BSD-103) cites research that basements can account for "upwards of 20 percent" of a house's heating and cooling load.

The slab's coolness also matters in summer. BSD-103 notes that the main risk is "warm moist air comes in contact with basement cold surfaces," which is why bare basement slabs sweat and why flooring over them can grow mold.

What Goes Under a New Slab?

BSD-103 lays out the layers from the ground up:

What's at the bottom?

A layer of crushed stone or coarse gravel, which acts as a capillary break so water doesn't wick up from the soil, and which can connect to the perimeter drain.

Where does the insulation go?

Rigid insulation goes on the stone, especially if the floor will have radiant heat, so the heat goes up into the room instead of down into the ground. Use foam rated for below-slab use with enough compressive strength for the slab load. XPS and EPS are both made in grades for this.

Where does the plastic go?

The vapor barrier goes on top of the insulation, in direct contact with the concrete. BSD-103 is firm that "a sand layer should never be installed" between the vapor barrier and the slab.

What about the edges?

Seal the slab to the perimeter wall. DOE's Building America Solution Center adds that slab edge insulation should run to the top of the slab to make a complete thermal break, since heat leaves fastest at the edge.

What About a Slab That's Already There?

You can't get under an existing slab without tearing it out, but you can insulate on top. A layer of rigid foam with a plywood or OSB subfloor screwed or floated over it, or an insulated subfloor panel, keeps finished flooring off the cold concrete and makes the floor feel warmer.

Before you do, make sure the slab doesn't take on water, and check headroom, stair heights and door clearances, since the floor will come up an inch or two.

What About Frost Heave?

Under a heated basement, the ground below the slab doesn't freeze. Frost heave matters for slabs that sit near the surface, like garages, porches and slab-on-grade additions.

For those, foam insulation placed around and outward from the slab edge keeps the ground underneath from freezing, a method called a frost-protected shallow foundation. Have it designed for your local frost depth and soil.

Seal and Insulate the Rim Joist

The rim joist sits right on the foundation, half above ground, with the cold siding on the other side. It leaks air at every seam and is cold enough in winter for moist basement air to condense on it.

What should I use?

BSD-103 says rim joists must be insulated with air-impermeable insulation. Fiberglass stuffed into the bays lets warm, moist air reach the cold wood, where it condenses. Use rigid foam cut to fit each bay with the edges sealed with spray foam or caulk, or spray foam itself.

Should I check anything first?

Make sure the rim isn't already wet. If water is getting in from outside, from a leaky deck ledger, gutters or grading, fix that first, or the foam will trap it against the wood.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should a basement slab have insulation under it?

Building Science Corporation's basement guide recommends rigid insulation under the slab, especially with radiant floor heat, along with a capillary break of crushed stone and a plastic vapor barrier directly under the concrete. It keeps the floor warmer, which also cuts summer condensation.

Should there be sand between the vapor barrier and the slab?

No. Building Science Corporation says a sand layer should never be installed between the vapor barrier and the slab. The vapor barrier goes in direct contact with the concrete.

Can I insulate an existing basement floor?

Yes, on top. A layer of rigid foam with a plywood or OSB subfloor over it, or an insulated subfloor panel, raises the floor surface and keeps finished flooring off the cold, damp slab. Check headroom and door clearances first.

What is the best way to insulate a rim joist?

Air-impermeable insulation, either rigid foam cut to fit each bay and sealed at the edges, or spray foam. Building Science Corporation says rim joists must be insulated with air-impermeable insulation, because fiberglass lets warm, moist air reach the cold wood.
