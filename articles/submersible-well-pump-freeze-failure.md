# No Water on a Frozen Morning? Well Pump, Pressure Tank and Pipe Freeze-Ups in Rural Midwest Homes

> **Quick Summary:** If you're on a private well and the taps go dry on a sub-zero morning, the pump down in the well probably isn't frozen. Groundwater deep down stays well above freezing. What freezes is near the surface: the little pipe to the pressure switch, the pressure tank, an exposed line in a crawlspace or pump house, or a wellhead without a pitless adapter. Shut off the pump's breaker, warm the frozen part gently, and then protect it for next time.
>
> - Turn off the pump's breaker before thawing anything.
> - Check the pressure switch nipple first. It's the most common spot.
> - Keep pumps and tanks above 40°F in an insulated space.
> - A pitless adapter keeps the well line below the frost.

---

If you're out in the country, your water comes from a pump, and when it's 20 below and nothing comes out of the faucet, it can feel like the whole system has failed. Usually it's one small part that froze, and you can find it.

## What Freezes First?

The pressure switch line.
The pressure switch tells the pump when to run. It sits on a short, thin pipe off the tank tee.

That small pipe holds very little water, so it freezes long before anything else. The Water Systems Council calls out that 1/4-inch nipple as a common freezing point and says to insulate it.

Why does that stop the water?
When that pipe freezes, the switch can't sense pressure, so it never tells the pump to turn on. The tank empties and the taps go dry, even though the well is fine.

Other usual suspects are the pressure tank itself in a cold room, an exposed pipe in a crawlspace or along an outside wall, and an older wellhead or pump house where the pipe comes above ground.

## What Do I Do Right Now?

Turn off the pump's breaker.
The Water Systems Council says to leave it off until the pressure switch line has thawed and is working again. A pump that runs against ice, or with no water reaching the switch, can damage itself.

Warm the frozen part gently.
Use an electric heating pad, a hair dryer, or a space heater kept well away from anything that can burn. No torches.

Start with the pressure switch line and the tank area, then follow the pipe toward the wall.

Look for leaks before you restore power.
Once things thaw, watch for drips or spraying. If a pipe or tank has split, or water is still not coming, call a licensed well contractor or plumber.

## How Do I Keep It From Happening Again?

Keep the equipment warm.
The Water Systems Council says an above-ground pump should stay where it's above 40°F, in an insulated enclosure if it drops below freezing, and that tanks should be in an insulated space too. A pump house with a thermostat-controlled heater, or a heat lamp that's safely mounted, is common in the Dakotas and Minnesota.

Insulate the small pipes.
Wrap the pressure switch nipple and any exposed lines. For cold crawlspaces, heating cable under insulation works, but follow CPSC's rules for heat tape: GFCI outlet, never over itself, and inspected every year.

Check for a pitless adapter.
A pitless adapter connects the water line to the side of the well casing below the frost line, so the pipe to the house runs underground the whole way. If your well still has an old pit or above-ground pump house, ask a licensed well contractor whether one makes sense.

Keep water moving during a cold snap.
Letting the faucet farthest from the tank drip during a deep freeze keeps water moving through the lines, and moving water is less likely to freeze.

## What If the Power Goes Out?

No power, no pump, no water. A long outage in the cold can freeze the whole house too. If you're on a well, plan for a backup power source that can run the pump.

Take a flashlight to the tank and the pressure switch before the next cold snap, and wrap anything that's bare. If you're not sure what you're looking at, a well contractor can check the whole setup in one visit.
