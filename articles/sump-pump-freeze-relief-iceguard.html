Frozen Sump Pump Discharge Line: How to Thaw It and Keep It From Freezing Again

A sump pump that runs in January can freeze its own discharge line, then burn itself out pushing against the ice. Here is how to tell, what to do, and how freeze-relief fittings and hose setups prevent it.

By Midwest AEC Building Science • Published November 2026 • NDSU AE1573 / Fargo sump pump program / Sioux Falls sump discharge guide
Quick Summary

In winter, a sump pump can still run, since groundwater doesn't stop when it gets cold. The water it pushes out can freeze at the end of the line, especially in a low spot, a long hose or a snowbank. Then the pump runs against a plug of ice until it overheats. Clear the outlet, give the line a steady slope so it empties, and add a freeze-relief fitting so water has a way out even if the end freezes.

  • A frozen line looks like a pump that runs but doesn't empty the pit.
  • Unplug the pump while you thaw the line.
  • Slope the line so it drains completely between cycles.
  • A freeze-relief fitting gives water a way out if the end freezes.

If your sump pump is running and running in the middle of winter, or the pit is full and the pump is just humming, go look at where the pipe comes out of the house. There's a good chance it's packed with ice or buried in snow.

Why Does It Freeze?

Each time the pump shuts off, whatever water is left in the line just sits there. If the pipe sags or runs uphill anywhere, water collects in the low spot and freezes. Each cycle adds a little more ice, like a gutter that slowly fills with icicles, until the line is closed off.

The end of the line is the most common spot. A long hose lying in a snowbank, or a buried pipe with an outlet that's iced over, can freeze shut on a cold night.

What Do I Do If It's Frozen?

Unplug the pump first.

Pushing against ice can overheat the motor. NDSU Extension says to unplug it, let it cool and clear the blockage, and the motor's thermal relay usually resets in 15 to 30 minutes.

Clear the ice.

Break the ice at the outlet, pull the hose free, or warm the frozen section with warm water or a hair dryer (keep the cord and dryer away from the water). Don't use an open flame on plastic pipe.

Keep the basement dry meanwhile.

If the pit is close to overflowing, a temporary hose run out a basement window or door, laid downhill, buys you time.

How Do I Keep It From Freezing?

Let it drain completely.

The line should slope down all the way from the house to the outlet, with no dips, so it empties after every cycle. Water that drains away can't freeze inside the pipe.

Keep the winter hose short.

Fargo's sump pump program tells homeowners not to leave a long hose connected in winter. It suggests an 8 to 10 foot hose on ground that slopes away from the house, and says snow over the hose actually helps insulate it.

Check it for kinks.

Add a freeze-relief fitting.

This is a fitting placed outside, just past where the pipe leaves the wall, with an opening that lets water spill out if the line beyond it freezes. The pump keeps working, and the water lands near the house instead of backing up into the basement.

Grade the ground there away from the foundation so it doesn't come right back in.

Where Should the Water Go?

Most of the time, onto your own yard, well away from the house. NDSU Extension and Fargo both point to about 20 feet, and Fargo adds: never into a neighbor's yard or across a public walkway.

What about the sewer?

Most cities don't allow sump water in the sanitary sewer, since clean groundwater overloads the treatment plant and adds to sewer backups. Some allow a winter exception.

Sioux Falls, for example, issues permits for winter discharge to the sanitary sewer to stop street icing. Ask your city before you change anything.

Before the Spring Thaw

Winter freezes are one problem. The bigger test comes when the snow melts and the ground is still frozen. Test the pump by pouring water into the pit, and think about a battery backup.

A quick walk outside to check the outlet after every big snow or cold snap is the easiest thing you can do. If the line is buried or sags, a waterproofing contractor can reroute it with a steady slope.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know my sump pump discharge line is frozen?

The pump runs but the water level in the pit doesn't drop, or the pump hums and shuts off, and nothing comes out outside. Ice or snow packed at the outlet is the usual cause.

What is a sump pump freeze-relief fitting?

A fitting placed outside, where the pipe leaves the house, with an opening that lets water spill out if the line beyond it is frozen. The water lands near the house instead of backing up, so slope the ground away from the foundation there.

Can I run my sump pump into the sanitary sewer in winter?

Usually not. Most cities ban it, because clean groundwater overloads sewers and treatment plants. Sioux Falls, for example, allows it only with a city permit to prevent street icing, so check with your city first.

How far from the house should a sump pump discharge?

NDSU Extension and Fargo both point to about 20 feet, onto ground that slopes away from the house. Fargo suggests a short 8 to 10 foot hose in winter rather than a long one that can kink or freeze.
