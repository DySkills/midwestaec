# Frost Adfreeze Shear Stress: How Saturated Topsoil Lifts Brick Foundations

In the northern reaches of Climate Zones 5A and 6A, winter frost depths commonly reach 42 to 60 inches below finished grade. While standard building codes mandate that foundation footings sit below this seasonal frost line to prevent bottom-up frost heaving, thousands of Midwest homes develop severe horizontal foundation cracks, racked window openings, and stair-step mortar separations every January and February.

The root mechanism is not footing heave, but **adfreeze shear stress**. When fine-grained, moisture-saturated clay and silt soils freeze solid against rough foundation surfaces (such as porous brick, rough concrete block, or textured concrete), the frozen soil bonds to the foundation stem wall. As frost penetrates deeper, thermal gradients draw subsurface water upward into expanding ice lenses, lifting the bonded soil. The vertical heave forces exceed the foundation's dead-load weight and the tensile capacity of masonry mortar, shearing stem walls horizontally along their bed joints.

## 1. Geotechnical Mechanics of Cryosuction and Adfreeze Shear

Adfreeze heave is governed by soil mechanics, heat transfer, and surface friction thermodynamics. The phenomenon requires three conditions: frost-susceptible soil (silty clays or fine sands), an accessible water table or perched water source, and freezing surface temperatures.

### Cryosuction and Ice Lens Segregation
Contrary to popular belief, soil heaving is not simply the 9 percent volumetric expansion of water freezing in soil pores. In fine-grained soils (classified under ASTM D2487 as CL, CH, or ML), capillary suction forces (cryosuction) pull unfrozen moisture from several feet below upward toward the advancing freezing front.

As moisture reaches the freezing isotherm, it segregates into horizontal, pure ice lenses. As long as water feeds the freezing zone, ice lenses grow continuously, producing vertical surface displacements of 2 to 8 inches:

$$q_w = -k_h \cdot \frac{d\psi}{dz}$$

Where:
* $q_w$ is the upward water flux feeding the growing ice lens.
* $k_h$ is the hydraulic conductivity of the unsaturated frozen fringe.
* $\frac{d\psi}{dz}$ is the suction potential gradient driven by the thermal depression below $32^\circ\text{F}$.

### Adfreeze Bond Strength and Masonry Tensile Failure
As saturated soil freezes in contact with a porous foundation surface, ice crystals penetrate the microscopic surface voids of the brick or concrete block, creating an adhesive shear bond known as the **adfreeze bond strength** ($\tau_{\text{ad}}$).

Empirical geotechnical measurements by the U.S. Army Cold Regions Research and Engineering Laboratory (CRREL) show that adfreeze bond strength on rough concrete and porous masonry ranges from **20 to 45 PSI** ($140\text{ to }310\text{ kPa}$).

The total upward vertical shearing force ($F_{\text{ad}}$) exerted on a foundation perimeter is calculated by integrating the adfreeze shear stress over the contact surface area within the frost zone:

$$F_{\text{ad}} = \tau_{\text{ad}} \cdot P \cdot D_f$$

Where:
* $P$ is the perimeter length of the foundation wall (in inches).
* $D_f$ is the active depth of frozen soil bonded to the wall (in inches).

For an uninsulated single-story residential home with an 80-foot exterior stem wall section and an active frost depth of 36 inches ($D_f = 36$):

$$F_{\text{ad}} = 30\text{ PSI} \cdot (80\text{ ft} \cdot 12\text{ in/ft}) \cdot 36\text{ in} = 30 \cdot 960 \cdot 36 = 1,036,800\text{ lbs} \approx 518\text{ tons}$$

This upward thrust of over 500 tons easily overcomes the downward gravity dead load of a light-frame residential wall (typically only 400 to 800 lbs per linear foot, or 16 to 32 tons total). 

Because masonry possesses high compressive strength but almost zero tensile bond strength (standard ASTM C270 Type N mortar has a flexural tensile capacity of just 25 to 40 PSI), the wall snaps horizontally along a bed joint 12 to 24 inches below grade. The top half of the foundation lifts upward, opening a 1/4-inch to 1/2-inch crack through which water pours during the spring thaw.

## 2. Why National Contractor Portals Fail Homeowners

Mainstream home repair directories provide misleading advice that exacerbates adfreeze foundation damage:

1. **Recommending Carbon Fiber Straps for Winter Cracking:** Carbon fiber straps (applied to the inside basement face) are designed to resist *lateral soil pressure* (inward bowing). They provide almost zero resistance against *vertical tensile uplifting* caused by adfreeze shear. Installing carbon fiber on an adfreeze-heaving wall results in the straps delaminating or snapping at the horizontal crack line.
2. **Advising Homeowners to Fill Exterior Cracks with Hydraulic Cement:** Chipping out the crack and packing it with hydraulic cement while the ground is frozen seals the opening temporarily. However, when the soil thaws in spring and the upper foundation settles back down, the rigid cement plug prevents the wall from closing, creating point-load stress concentrations that shatter the surrounding brick.
3. **Ignoring Topsoil Permeability and Surface Grading:** Aggregators routinely treat foundation cracks as structural failures requiring deep helical piers. If adfreeze shear is the root cause, helical piers installed to the footing do nothing, because the footing is already stationary. The heave is occurring above the footing in the frozen stem wall.

## 3. Forensic Diagnostic Decision Matrix

Use this forensic protocol to distinguish between true footing frost heave, lateral hydrostatic bowing, and vertical adfreeze shear.

| Diagnostic Test | Observed Condition | Engineering Failure Mode | Targeted Remediation Strategy |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| **01** | Horizontal crack 12-24 inches below grade; opens in winter, partially closes in spring | Vertical adfreeze shear bond lifting the upper stem wall | Install vertical slip-plane membrane (Delta-MS) and non-frost-susceptible backfill. |
| **02** | Center of basement wall bows inward; horizontal crack at mid-height; wall leans | Lateral hydrostatic and clay swelling pressure | Install interior carbon fiber grid straps or structural steel I-beam soldiers. |
| **03** | Vertical stair-step crack originating at footing; building corner drops 1-2 inches | Footing settlement due to soil consolidation or footing above frost line | Install deep hydraulically driven steel push piers or helical underpinning to bedrock. |
| **04** | Foundation lifts uniformly around unheated detached garage or slab-on-grade | Frost penetration beneath shallow monolithic slab edges | Retrofit frost-protected shallow foundation (FPSF) horizontal rigid XPS insulation skirts. |
| **05** | Mortar spalls and crumbles on exterior brick surface within 6 inches of soil | Freeze-thaw spalling from saturated clay soil contact and salt crystallization | Regrade to maintain minimum 6-inch clearance between soil and brick masonry. |

### Protocol A: Slip-Plane Membrane and Friction Isolation
1. Excavate the exterior foundation perimeter down to 12 inches below the local frost depth (typically 48 to 60 inches below grade).
2. Clean the masonry surface using a medium-pressure water wash to remove all loose mortar and soil. Repair cracked bed joints using an ASTM C270 Type N mortar mix matching the historic compressive strength.
3. Apply a continuous elastomeric waterproofing membrane (ASTM C836) directly to the exterior masonry surface to block capillary water absorption.
4. Install a high-density polyethylene (HDPE) dimpled drainage membrane (such as Dörken Delta-MS). Install the dimples facing inward toward the wall. The smooth outer plastic face acts as a permanent low-friction slip plane. When surrounding soil freezes and heaves upward, it slides harmlessly across the slick HDPE face without transferring shear stress to the masonry wall.

### Protocol B: Non-Frost-Susceptible (NFS) Backfill Replacement
1. Discard the excavated high-plasticity clay and silt backfill.
2. Backfill the excavation with washed, non-frost-susceptible coarse sand or crushed gravel (ASTM C33 concrete sand or AASHTO No. 57 stone) containing less than 3 percent fines passing the No. 200 sieve ($0.075\text{ mm}$). Non-frost-susceptible granular materials cannot support capillary cryosuction, eliminating ice lens formation against the foundation.
3. Terminate the gravel backfill 6 inches below finished grade. Place a non-woven geotextile filter fabric (Class 1, AASHTO M288) over the gravel, and cap with 6 inches of low-permeability clayey topsoil sloped at a 5 percent grade (6 inches of fall over the first 10 feet) away from the structure.

## 6. Building Code Citations and Geotechnical Standards

* **International Residential Code (IRC) Section R403.1.4.1:** Dictates exterior footings shall be placed below the frost line specified in Table R301.2, or protected from frost heave in accordance with ASCE 32.
* **ASCE Standard 32-01 (Design and Construction of Frost-Protected Shallow Foundations):** Engineering requirements for using ground insulation geometry to prevent sub-grade soil freezing adjacent to structural foundations.
* **ACI Standard 530/530.1 (Building Code Requirements for Masonry Structures):** Prescribes minimum tensile and compressive limits for masonry units and mortar bed joints under combined dead and lateral/uplift loads.
* **ASTM D2487 (Standard Practice for Classification of Soils for Engineering Purposes):** Engineering criteria identifying frost-susceptible silts and clays prone to cryosuction ice segregation.
