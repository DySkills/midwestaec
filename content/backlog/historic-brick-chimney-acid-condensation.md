# Historic Brick Chimney Deterioration: Flue Acid Condensation and Spalling Mortar Crowns

> **Quick Summary:** Across Midwest cities like Chicago, St. Louis, Milwaukee, and Minneapolis, tens of thousands of historic masonry chimneys built between 1890 and 1950 are rapidly crumbling from the inside out. Homeowners frequently assume exterior brick spalling is caused by exterior weather exposure. In reality, the primary culprit is often mechanical: retrofitting an 80% or 90%+ AFUE modern furnace or water heater into an existing oversized, unlined clay tile chimney. Low exhaust temperatures cause water vapor and sulfur oxides to condense along the cold interior chimney walls, forming dilute sulfuric and nitric acids that chemically attack soft historic lime mortar.
>
> - Modern high-efficiency furnaces produce flue exhaust temperatures of 120 to 140 deg F, far below the 350 to 500 deg F required to keep moisture vaporized in a 30-foot tall outdoor brick chase.
> - Acidic flue condensate (pH 3.0 to 4.5) dissolves the calcium carbonate binder in historic Type O and lime mortars, turning interior joints into damp sand.
> - Flat mortar wash chimney caps crack within 3 to 5 winter seasons; code-compliant cast-in-place concrete crowns require a minimum 2-inch overhang with a continuous drip edge kerf under IRC R1003.20.
> - Remediation requires installing an insulated UL 1777 listed stainless steel flue liner sized precisely to the appliance BTU input, isolating flue gases from the brickwork permanently.

## The Thermodynamics of Flue Gas Condensation

When natural gas burns, the combustion reaction produces water vapor and carbon dioxide:
```
CH_4 + 2 O_2 ---> CO_2 + 2 H_2O + Heat
```
For every 100,000 BTUs of natural gas consumed, approximately 1 gallon of water vapor is generated. In legacy gravity heating systems, exhaust gases left the appliance at 400 to 600 deg F. This high thermal energy carried the moisture safely out the chimney termination before flue gas temperatures dropped below the dew point (approximately 130 deg F).

Modern appliances extract this sensible heat to achieve higher efficiency. When an 80% AFUE furnace or a modern water heater discharges exhaust at 250 to 300 deg F into an exterior 8-by-12-inch uninsulated brick chimney, the large thermal mass of the cold brick rapidly cools the rising gas column. By the time the exhaust reaches the upper third of the chimney, gas temperatures drop below the 130 deg F dew point.

Moisture condenses in liquid droplets along the clay tile or raw brick. Sulfur compounds in the gas dissolve in this water to create sulfuric acid:
```
SO_2 + H_2O ---> H_2SO_3 (Sulfurous Acid)
H_2SO_3 + 1/2 O_2 ---> H_2SO_4 (Sulfuric Acid)
```
This acidic solution penetrates masonry mortar joints. Historic lime mortar relies on calcium carbonate (CaCO_3) for strength. The sulfuric acid reacts directly with calcium carbonate:
```
CaCO_3 + H_2SO_4 ---> CaSO_4 (Gypsum) + H_2O + CO_2
```
Gypsum has no binding strength in this wet environment and expands substantially, spalling the brick faces from behind. Homeowners discover white salt deposits and sand accumulation in their cleanout doors, signaling structural decay.

## IRC R1003.20 Concrete Crown vs. Mortar Wash

The single most common exterior failure on Midwest chimneys is the traditional mortar wash. Masons frequently finish the chimney top by slanting a thin layer of leftover bricklaying mortar from the clay flue tile to the brick edge. Mortar shrinks as it cures, lacks reinforcing fibers, and holds zero tensile strength against sub-zero freezing.

```
       INCORRECT: MORTAR WASH                  CORRECT: OVERHANGING CONCRETE CROWN
    (Water runs down chimney face)               (Drip edge casts water clear)

          [ Flue Tile ]                                [ Flue Tile ]
             │     │                                      │     │
       ┌─────┴─────┴─────┐                                │     │
       │  Thin Mortar    │                      ┌─────────┴─────┴─────────┐
       │     Wash        │                      │ 3" Cast Concrete Crown  │
       └─────┬─────┬─────┘                      └────┬───────────────┬────┘
             │     │                                 │ ◄─Drip Kerf─► │  (2" Overhang)
       ┌─────┴─────┴─────┐                      ┌────┴───────────────┴────┐
       │  Upper Brick    │                      │  Upper Brick Courses    │
       │  Courses        │                      │  (Protected from water) │
       │  (Spalling)     │                      │                         │
```

IRC Section R1003.20 explicitly mandates a proper chimney crown:
1. **Material:** Minimum 3-inch thick reinforced 3,000-psi Portland concrete (not masonry mortar).
2. **Overhang:** Must overhang the brick walls by a minimum of 2 inches on all four sides.
3. **Drip Edge:** Continuous 1/4-inch deep drip kerf cast into the underside of the overhang, preventing runoff from tracking back against the masonry.
4. **Expansion Joint:** 3/8-inch flexible, high-temperature silicone or polyurethane joint between the crown concrete and the clay flue tile, allowing thermal expansion without cracking the crown.

## Step-by-Step Chimney Remediation Protocol

1. **NFPA 211 Level II Video Inspection:** Run a continuous drop-camera down the flue to inspect every terra cotta tile joint for acid degradation, cracking, and missing refractory mortar.
2. **Stainless Steel Flue Relining:** Install an insulated 316Ti or AL 29-4C stainless steel flexible or rigid liner sized to appliance input per NFPA 54 / NFPA 211. Wrap the liner with 1/2-inch ceramic fiber insulation blanket to maintain draft temperatures and isolate heat from surrounding woodwork.
3. **Mortar Joint Raking & Repointing:** Grind deteriorated exterior mortar joints to a minimum depth of 3/4 inch using diamond tuckpointing blades. Never use hard Type S Portland mortar; repoint historic clay brick with vapor-permeable Type O or natural hydraulic lime (NHL 3.5) mortar per ASTM C270.
4. **Cast-in-Place Concrete Crown Installation:** Build wood edge forms on top of the chimney with 2-inch overhangs. Lay welded wire mesh reinforcement and pour a 3-to-4-inch monolithic concrete crown sloped at 1/4 inch per foot to the edges.
5. **Siloxane Water-Repellent Application:** Once mortar cures (28 days), apply a 100% breathable silane-siloxane penetrating water repellent (such as Prosoco Siloxane WB) to protect soft brick while allowing interior moisture to dry outward.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I vent a new 90%+ furnace through my old brick chimney?
No. High-efficiency condensing furnaces (90%+ AFUE) produce liquid acidic condensate and cold flue gases that cannot maintain draft in a masonry chimney. They must vent through dedicated Category IV PVC, CPVC, or polypropylene piping directly through a sidewall or roof penetration. If you abandon the furnace in the chimney and leave only an atmospheric water heater, you must install an insulated stainless steel liner to prevent the smaller water heater flue gases from condensing in the oversized flue (the "orphan water heater" problem).

### How much does it cost to reline a brick chimney with stainless steel?
Professional installation of a UL 1777 insulated stainless steel chimney liner typically costs between $1,800 and $3,400 for a standard two-story Midwest residential home. If exterior masonry repointing, brick replacement, and a new concrete crown are required, total restoration ranges from $3,500 to $7,500 depending on scaffolding access and chimney height.

### Why do brick faces flake off near the top of the chimney?
Brick flaking (spalling) occurs when moisture saturates soft clay brick and freezes. Water expands by 9.05% upon freezing. When internal pore pressures exceed the tensile strength of the brick (typically 150 to 300 psi for historic common brick), the outer 1/4 to 1/2 inch of the brick face shears off. This is accelerated when leaking crowns allow water to saturate the brick from above or unlined furnaces saturate it from inside.
