# Mineral Wool Soundproofing in Floor Joists: STC Ratings and Non-Combustible Firestopping

In modern multi-story residential homes, finished basements, and multi-family duplexes across the Midwest, two of the most frequent owner complaints center on acoustic privacy and fire safety. Footstep thuds, voices, and home theater bass reverberate through open floor-ceiling assemblies, while concealed joist cavities create vertical pathways that allow house fires to spread undetected across entire floor levels in minutes.

Contractors frequently attempt to resolve sound transmission by stuffing standard pink fiberglass batt insulation between the floor joists before hanging drywall. In more than 80 percent of post-construction acoustic tests, this fiberglass-only approach fails to satisfy occupant expectations. 

Fiberglass is lightweight, lacks sufficient mass to dampen low-frequency impact noise, and melts at temperatures commonly reached in residential structure fires ($1,050 degrees Fahrenheit).

The professional architectural specification to solve both challenges simultaneously is **high-density mineral wool insulation (spun basalt stone and blast furnace slag, such as Rockwool Safe'n'Sound)** installed within an engineered resilient channel floor-ceiling assembly. 

Mineral wool provides superior acoustic mass, eliminates cavity resonance, and provides a **non-combustible firestop barrier that withstands temperatures exceeding 2,150 degrees Fahrenheit**.

## 1. Architectural Acoustics: Mass-Air-Mass Resonance Physics

Sound transmission through a building assembly occurs primarily via airborne sound (voices, television, music) and structure-borne impact noise (footsteps, moving furniture, dropped objects).

### Sound Transmission Class (STC) and Damping
The acoustic resistance of a wall or ceiling assembly to airborne noise is rated by the **Sound Transmission Class (STC)** under ASTM E90:

* **STC 30 to 35:** Loud speech can be easily understood through the ceiling.
* **STC 40 to 45:** Loud speech heard as a murmur; television audible.
* **STC 50 to 55:** Loud speech completely inaudible; standard for luxury multi-family construction (IBC Section 1206.2).
* **STC 58+:** Exceptional acoustic isolation; musical instruments suppressed.

A standard floor assembly behaves as a classic **Mass-Air-Mass acoustic resonance system**: the subfloor is the first mass, the hollow joist cavity is the acoustic spring, and the ceiling drywall is the second mass.

At the assembly's natural resonance frequency ($f_0$):

$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{\rho_{\text{air}} \cdot c^2}{d} \cdot \left(\frac{1}{m_1} + \frac{1}{m_2}\right)}$$

Where:
* $m_1$ and $m_2$ are the surface densities of the subfloor and drywall ($\text{lbs/sq ft}$).
* $d$ is the joist cavity depth.

At this resonance frequency (typically $60\text{ to }125\text{ Hz}$), the hollow joist bay acts as an acoustic drum, amplifying low-frequency thuds and bass notes!

### The Physics of Mineral Wool vs Fiberglass
1. **Material Density (Mass Law):** Under the acoustic Mass Law, sound transmission loss increases by roughly 6 decibels for every doubling of material surface mass. Standard fiberglass batts have a low density of only **$0.5\text{ to }0.7\text{ lbs per cubic foot (lbs/cu ft)}$**. Mineral wool is manufactured from molten basalt volcanic rock spun into dense fibers, yielding a material density of **$2.5\text{ to }3.0\text{ lbs/cu ft}$** (more than four times denser than fiberglass).
2. **Noise Reduction Coefficient (NRC):** Mineral wool possesses an exceptional Noise Reduction Coefficient (ASTM C423) of **$\text{NRC} = 1.05$** across mid and high frequencies. Its dense, tortuous fiber matrix traps sound waves, converting acoustic wave kinetic energy into microscopic heat friction through viscous drag in its pore network.
3. **Mechanical Decoupling with Resilient Channels:** High mass alone cannot stop structure-borne footstep impact noise (Impact Insulation Class, IIC). Sound vibrations travel mechanically through the solid wood joists directly into the drywall. Fastening the drywall to steel **resilient channels (RC-1 or RC-Deluxe)** spaced across the joists breaks the physical vibration path, boosting the assembly's overall rating by **12 to 15 STC points**.

### The Non-Combustible Fire Barrier
Standard fiberglass insulation is manufactured using recycled glass silica, which melts and collapses into a liquid puddle at **$1,050^\circ\text{F}$**. In a real residential structure fire, temperatures easily exceed $1,400^\circ\text{F}$ within 8 to 12 minutes. The fiberglass vanishes, exposing the structural wood joists directly to the flames.

Mineral wool is classified as **non-combustible under ASTM E136**. Because it is made of natural basalt rock, its melting point exceeds **$2,150^\circ\text{F}\text{ ($1,177^\circ\text{C}$)}$**, which is higher than the flame temperature of a standard building fire.

In full-scale fire endurance testing under **ASTM E119**, a floor-ceiling assembly packed with 3 inches of mineral wool and covered with 5/8-inch Type X gypsum board earns a verified **1-Hour to 2-Hour Fire-Resistance Rating**, delaying structural collapse and preventing fire from leaping between floor levels.

## 3. Why DIY Aggregators Fail Homeowners

Mainstream home improvement articles provide flawed instructions that destroy soundproofing performance:

1. **Short-Circuiting Resilient Channels with Long Screws:** Handymen frequently install resilient channels correctly, but then use 2-inch or 2-1/2-inch drywall screws to hang the ceiling gypsum. If a screw passes through the channel and bites into the wooden joist above, it creates a rigid mechanical bridge. A single short-circuited screw destroys the acoustic decoupling, dropping the assembly's STC rating by **8 to 10 points**!
2. **Compressing Mineral Wool Too Tightly:** Installers sometimes force 6 inches of dense mineral wool into a 4-inch cavity, believing more compression yields better soundproofing. Over-compressing dense batts creates a rigid physical link that mechanically couples the subfloor to the ceiling drywall, transmitting impact vibrations right through the insulation!
3. **Leaving Perimeter Acoustic Flanking Gaps:** Sound travels through air gaps like water through a sieve. If the installer fails to seal the perimeter gap between the ceiling drywall and the adjacent walls with non-hardening acoustical caulk, sound flanking through the unsealed crack reduces the entire room's sound isolation by more than **50 percent**.

### Installation Standard (ASTM E90 & IBC Chapter 12)
1. **Mineral Wool Batting Layout:**
   * Install 3-inch thick high-density mineral wool sound and fire batts (such as Rockwool Safe'n'Sound) into the floor joist bays.
   * Friction-fit the batts between the joists, ensuring snug contact against both joist faces without over-compression.
   * Leave an intentional **1-inch to 2-inch open air space** between the top of the mineral wool and the underside of the subfloor. This maintains the essential mass-air-mass acoustic spring, optimizing low-frequency sound absorption.
2. **Resilient Channel Mechanical Decoupling:**
   * Fasten 25-gauge hemmed steel resilient channels (ClarkDietrich RC-Deluxe) perpendicular to the floor joists at **16 inches on center** (or 12 inches on center for double-layer drywall).
   * Fasten the mounting flange to each joist using a single 1-1/4-inch Type W drywall screw. Install all channels with the open mounting leg facing the same direction.
3. **Drywall Fastening and Screw Length Control:**
   * Install 5/8-inch Type X fire-rated gypsum board perpendicular to the resilient channels, staggering end joints by at least 4 feet.
   * **The Fastener Rule:** Fasten drywall using **1-1/4-inch Type S fine-thread drywall screws**. The screw length must be precisely calculated: $5/8\text{-inch drywall} + 1/2\text{-inch channel depth} = 1-1/8\text{ inches}$. The screw penetrates the channel flange, but **never touches the wooden joist above**, preserving 100 percent mechanical decoupling.
4. **Perimeter Acoustic Caulking:**
   * Leave a continuous 1/4-inch expansion perimeter gap between the edges of the ceiling drywall and the surrounding wall framing.
   * Fill this gap with a continuous, heavy bead of non-hardening, non-skinning synthetic acoustical sealant (ASTM C919, such as Tremco Acoustical Sealant or OSI SC-175). Cover with standard paper drywall tape and joint compound to form an airtight acoustic and smoke-stop seal.

## 6. Building Code Citations and Acoustic Standards

* **International Building Code (IBC) Section 1206.2 (Airborne Sound):** Mandates that walls, partitions, and floor-ceiling assemblies separating dwelling units shall have a sound transmission class (STC) of not less than 50.
* **IBC Section 1206.3 (Structure-Borne Sound):** Requires floor-ceiling assemblies between dwelling units to achieve an Impact Insulation Class (IIC) of not less than 50.
* **ASTM E119:** Standard Test Methods for Fire Tests of Building Construction and Materials.
* **ASTM C612:** Standard Specification for Mineral Fiber Block and Board Thermal Insulation.
