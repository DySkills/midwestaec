# Sunken Concrete Slabs: Polyurethane Polyjacking vs Mudjacking for Settled Walkways and Driveways

Throughout the Midwest, exterior concrete flatwork (driveway slabs, sidewalk panels, front porch landings, and garage apron approaches) frequently settles and tilts following spring thaws and heavy rains. When a concrete walkway sinks 1 to 3 inches toward a house foundation, it creates an aggressive reverse slope that channels thousands of gallons of surface runoff directly into the basement wall, while creating severe pedestrian trip hazards that violate municipal liability codes.

Contractors offer two primary methods to raise and stabilize sunken slabs without the expense of full concrete replacement: **traditional mudjacking (hydraulic pressure grouting with a cement-and-soil slurry)** and **polyjacking (injecting two-part high-density expanding structural polyurethane foam)**.

While mudjacking has been practiced for over 80 years, forensic geotechnical engineering reveals that injecting tons of heavy cement slurry often accelerates long-term settlement in poorly compacted soils. Polyurethane polyjacking offers superior geotechnical performance due to its lightweight density, hydrophobic chemistry, and rapid cure times.

## 1. Geotechnical Mechanics of Slab Settlement and Soil Consolidation

Concrete slabs do not sink because the concrete is defective; they sink because the subgrade soil supporting them consolidates, erodes, or shrinks.

### Subgrade Consolidation and Soil Overburden
Around residential foundations, concrete porches, walkways, and driveways are frequently poured over uncompacted backfill soil within the "clay bowl" excavation trench. When spring rainwater infiltrates the loose backfill, the soil particles rearrange into a denser packing state (consolidation), leaving a subterranean void beneath the rigid concrete slab.

Once the void spans more than 4 to 6 feet, the slab can no longer support its own dead weight (roughly **50 PSF for a standard 4-inch slab**). The concrete cracks and settles into the void.

### The Physics of Mudjacking Slurry (Mass Surcharge)
Traditional mudjacking pumps a semi-liquid slurry composed of topsoil, clay, sand, and Portland cement through large **2-inch diameter holes** cored through the concrete. The hydraulic pressure of the slurry pushes the slab upward.

However, mudjacking slurry is extremely heavy, with a wet cured density ($\rho_{\text{mud}}$) of **100 to 120 pounds per cubic foot (lbs/cu ft)**. 

To lift a sunken two-car garage apron that has dropped 2 inches over an 8-foot by 16-foot area ($128\text{ sq ft}$), a mudjacking crew must inject roughly 21 cubic feet of slurry:

$$W_{\text{slurry}} = 21.3\text{ cu ft} \cdot 110\text{ lbs/cu ft} = 2,343\text{ pounds}$$

The contractor has just placed more than **1.1 tons of additional dead-load weight** on top of the exact same weak, consolidating subgrade soil that caused the slab to sink in the first place! 

Furthermore, because mudjacking slurry contains natural topsoil and sand held together by modest amounts of Portland cement, it is vulnerable to **sub-surface erosion**. When spring groundwater flows beneath the slab, the slurry dissolves and washes away, causing the slab to settle again within two to three years.

### The Polymer Chemistry of Expanding Polyurethane (Polyjacking)
Polyjacking utilizes a closed-cell, two-part structural polyurethane system consisting of an "A-side" polymeric isocyanate (PMDI) and a "B-side" polyol resin blend containing catalysts and water-blown blowing agents:

$$\text{R-NCO (Isocyanate)} + \text{R'-OH (Polyol)} \to \text{Polyurethane Polymer} + \text{CO}_2\ (\text{Expansion Gas})$$

When the two liquids are impingement-mixed at high pressure ($1,000\text{ to }1,500\text{ PSI}$) inside a specialized injection gun, an exothermic polymerization reaction occurs within 10 to 15 seconds. The generated carbon dioxide gas causes the liquid to expand into a high-density, closed-cell rigid foam with an expansion ratio of **15:1 to 20:1**.

**Key Geotechnical Advantages of Polyurethane Foam:**
1. **Extreme Strength-to-Weight Ratio:** Cured polyjacking foam has a density of only **3.5 to 5.0 lbs/cu ft**, which is roughly **30 times lighter than mudjacking slurry**. For the same 128-square-foot garage apron, the total weight added to the subgrade is less than **90 pounds** (compared to 2,343 pounds of mud slurry), completely eliminating subgrade surcharge consolidation.
2. **High Hydraulic Lift Pressure:** As the closed-cell foam expands in the confined space between the subgrade and the slab, it generates expansive hydraulic pressures of **80 to 120 PSI (11,500 to 17,000 PSF)**. This powerful force easily lifts heavy structural slabs, while driving downward into the loose subgrade soil to compact and densify the native earth.
3. **Hydrophobic Chemistry:** Closed-cell polyurethane is completely hydrophobic (impervious to water absorption, ASTM D2842 $< 1.0\%$). It does not erode, dissolve, or shrink when exposed to running groundwater, spring runoff, or winter frost cycles.

## 2. Why Aggregator Directories Provide Outdated Advice

Mainstream contractor directories often treat mudjacking and polyjacking as equivalent options, differing only in price:

1. **Recommending Mudjacking Purely for Lower Initial Cost:** Aggregator articles often suggest mudjacking because it is 20 to 30 percent cheaper up front. They ignore the reality that mudjacking has a 40 percent failure rate within 36 months in cohesive Midwest clay soils, forcing the homeowner to pay for a second repair or complete concrete replacement.
2. **Ignoring Core Hole Aesthetics:** Mudjacking requires drilling massive 2-inch diameter holes across the slab surface (roughly the size of a soda can). Even when patched with gray cement, these giant circular plugs permanently disfigure stamped concrete, decorative patios, and broom-finished driveways. Polyjacking requires only 5/8-inch holes (the diameter of a penny), which blend invisibly into the concrete after patching.
3. **Underestimating Cure Times:** Mudjacking slurry requires 24 to 48 hours to cure before heavy vehicles can drive on the slab. High-density polyurethane foam reaches 90 percent of its structural compressive strength within **15 minutes** of injection, allowing driveways to be reopened to vehicular traffic immediately after the technician packs up.

### Injection Standard (ACI 546R & ICRI Guidelines)
1. **Subgrade Assessment and Laser Setup:**
   * Set up a high-precision rotating laser level and optical receiver rod to establish baseline slab elevations and determine the exact target lift height required to restore a minimum 2 percent slope away from the home.
2. **Drilling Injection Ports:**
   * Drill 5/8-inch diameter injection holes through the concrete slab using a rotary hammer drill equipped with a clean carbide bit and a vacuum attachment.
   * Space holes in an engineered grid pattern, typically 3 to 4 feet apart, located 12 to 18 inches from the edges of the slab to prevent edge blowouts.
3. **Polymer Injection Standard:**
   * Insert a mechanical injection port (brass or plastic drive injector) into each hole.
   * Connect the dual-component hydraulic proportioner gun (operating at $120^\circ\text{F}\text{ to }140^\circ\text{F}$ material temperature).
   * Inject high-density, closed-cell structural polyurethane foam meeting **ASTM D1621 (compressive strength $\ge 80\text{ to }100\text{ PSI}$)** and **ASTM D1622 (core density $\ge 3.5\text{ to }4.5\text{ lbs/cu ft}$)**.
   * Inject the foam in controlled, short bursts (3 to 5 seconds). Monitor the laser level continuously to observe the lift. As the foam expands, it initially fills subgrade voids; once voids are filled, it generates vertical lift, raising the slab smoothly without sudden shock.
4. **Port Patching and Cleanup:**
   * Remove the injection mechanical fittings.
   * Clean each 5/8-inch hole and patch flush with non-shrink, polymer-modified Portland cement repair mortar (ASTM C1107) color-matched to the surrounding concrete surface.
   * Reopen the slab to pedestrian foot traffic immediately, and to vehicular traffic within 30 minutes of project completion.
