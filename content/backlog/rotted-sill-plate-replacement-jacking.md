# Rotted Mud Sills and Rim Joists: House Jacking, Sistering and Sill Gasket Capillary Breaks

> **Quick Summary:** In older homes throughout the Midwest built before the widespread adoption of modern building codes, builders routinely laid untreated dimensional 2x6 or 2x8 lumber directly on top of wet concrete or limestone foundation walls without an impermeable capillary break. Concrete is a porous mineral sponge that draws groundwater up from the soil through capillary suction. Over decades, this continuous capillary moisture transfer keeps the wood mud sill plate and rim joist at a moisture content exceeding 28%, creating ideal conditions for wood-decay fungi (brown rot) and subterranean termite infestation.
>
> - Once wood moisture content exceeds 28% in temperatures above 40 deg F, wood-destroying fungi consume the structural cellulose matrix, reducing the crushing strength of the sill plate to zero.
> - A crushed, rotted sill plate causes the entire first-floor framing assembly to drop 1/2 to 2 inches, resulting in sloping floors, cracked interior drywall, jammed exterior doors, and exterior siding buckling.
> - Replacing a structural sill plate requires building a temporary heavy-timber load-bearing shoring wall in the basement to support the tributary floor and wall loads before jacking the house 1/8 to 1/4 inch with hydraulic bottle jacks.
> - Replacement lumber must be ground-contact pressure-treated lumber (AWPA Category UC4A) bedded over a continuous 1/4-inch closed-cell polyethylene foam sill gasket with 1/2-inch anchor bolts placed per IRC Section R403.1.6.

## The Physics of Capillary Wicking and Wood Rot

Concrete possesses an extensive microscopic pore network. Under capillary action, concrete draws groundwater upward from damp footing soils against gravity to heights of up to several hundred feet, behaving like a continuous liquid pump.

When an untreated Douglas fir or southern yellow pine 2x6 sill plate rests directly on this damp concrete, moisture wicks directly into the end grain and bottom face of the wood. Wood moisture content (MC) stabilizes based on surrounding humidity:
* Below 19% MC: Wood is structurally sound; fungi cannot germinate.
* 20% to 27% MC: Fiber saturation point; decay spores remain dormant but viable.
* 28%+ MC: Free water exists in cell cavities; brown rot fungi (*Gloeophyllum trabeum*) activate, secreting enzymes that consume cellulose and leave brittle, brown, cubically cracked lignin.

As the wood fibers degrade, the crushing strength perpendicular to grain drops from 600+ psi down to less than 50 psi. The weight of the two-story house crushes the rotted 2x6 plate flat, dropping the perimeter framing down onto the raw concrete.

## Step-by-Step House Jacking and Sill Plate Replacement Protocol

1. **Calculate Tributary Framing Load:** Calculate the tributary load supported by the affected foundation wall. A standard two-story Midwest home bears approximately 1,200 to 1,800 pounds per linear foot on exterior bearing walls.
2. **Erect Temporary Basement Shoring Wall:** Build a temporary heavy-timber 2x6 stud wall 24 to 36 inches back from the foundation wall. Install a double 2x8 top plate tight against the underside of the floor joists. Space 2x6 studs at 12 inches on center directly over a 2x8 bottom plate resting on the concrete basement floor slab. If the basement floor is a thin 2-inch slab, install temporary wood cribbing or screw jacks down to footings.
3. **Stage Hydraulic Bottle Jacks:** Position 20-ton hydraulic bottle jacks under the shoring beam spaced 6 to 8 feet apart. Rest jacks on 1/2-inch steel plates to distribute point loads.
4. **Execute Controlled Hydraulic Jacking:** Pump all hydraulic jacks simultaneously and incrementally. Lift the floor assembly no more than 1/8 to 1/4 inch; this provides just enough clearance to slide the old rotted wood out without cracking upstairs drywall or plumbing lines. Secure mechanical safety nuts or lock rings on the jacks.
5. **Extract Rotted Wood:** Saw through rusted anchor bolts using a reciprocating saw equipped with a carbide metal blade. Pry out the rotted sill plate and damaged band joist sections.
6. **Clean and Prepare Concrete Surface:** Scrape all rotted wood fibers, dirt, and mortar debris off the top of the concrete foundation stem wall. Coat the concrete with asphaltic damp-proofing or epoxy coating if significant efflorescence is present.
7. **Install Closed-Cell Sill Gasket:** Lay a continuous 5.5-inch wide by 1/4-inch thick ribbed closed-cell polyethylene foam sill gasket (such as Owens Corning FoamSealR) along the concrete wall. This provides an airtight thermal break and physically halts capillary moisture wicking.
8. **Install UC4A Pressure-Treated Sill Plate:** Install new #2 Southern Yellow Pine lumber pressure-treated with Copper Azole (CA-C) or Micronized Copper Quaternary (MCQ) rated for UC4A Ground Contact per AWPA standards.
9. **Anchor to Foundation:** Drill 1/2-inch holes through the new sill plate into the concrete with a hammer drill. Drive 1/2-inch by 8-inch zinc-plated Simpson Strong-Tie wedge anchors 4.5 inches deep into the concrete, spaced within 12 inches of each plate end and every 6 feet on center per IRC Section R403.1.6.
10. **Sister Damaged Joists & Lower House:** Where floor joist ends suffered rot, sister new full-depth lumber alongside the old joists for a minimum overlap of 4 feet, securing with structural timber screws (Simpson Strong-Drive SDWS). Slowly release the hydraulic jacks, transferring the home's weight onto the new, insulated, code-compliant sill plate assembly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I replace a rotted sill plate from the outside without going into the basement?
No. Attempting to replace a sill plate from the exterior without building a temporary basement shoring wall to support the floor joists will cause the first floor to collapse downward when the rotted wood is removed. The weight of the house must be transferred onto temporary basement shoring before any wood can be safely cut away.

### What kind of nails or screws must be used with modern pressure-treated lumber?
Modern pressure-treated lumber uses copper-based wood preservatives (ACQ, CA-B, MCQ) that are highly corrosive to standard steel fasteners. You must use hot-dipped galvanized fasteners conforming to ASTM A153 (minimum 1.0 oz of zinc per square foot) or Type 304/316 stainless steel fasteners. Using standard electro-galvanized framing nails will result in fastener corrosion within 5 to 7 years.

### How much does professional sill plate replacement cost?
Professional sill plate replacement typically costs between $120 and $250 per linear foot. A standard 20-foot rotted section averages $2,400 to $5,000, including temporary shoring, hydraulic jacking, new treated lumber, anchor bolting, and sistering damaged floor joists. If extensive siding removal or utility relocations (gas lines, electrical panels) are involved, costs can range from $6,000 to $12,000.
