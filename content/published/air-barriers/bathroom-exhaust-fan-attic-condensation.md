# Bathroom Fan Dripping Water? Why Exhaust Ducts Condense in a Cold Attic

> **Quick Summary:** In winter, a bath fan pushes warm, wet shower air up through a duct in a freezing attic. If that duct is uninsulated, sagging or running uphill, the moisture condenses inside it, like your breath fogging a cold window, and the water runs back down to the fan and drips on you. The fix is in the attic: a short, smooth, insulated duct that slopes to the outside and has a damper at the end.
>
> - Dripping after showers is likely condensation, not a roof leak.
> - The duct must end outside, never in the attic.
> - Use smooth duct, insulate it, and slope it toward the outside.
> - Run the fan 15 minutes to an hour after showering.

---

If water is dripping out of the bathroom fan grille, or there's a brown ring on the ceiling around it, it's easy to assume the roof is leaking. In a Midwest winter, the shower is a more likely culprit.

## Roof Leak or Condensation?

When does it drip?
If it drips during or right after showers, and on the coldest days, it's most likely condensation. If it drips on a sunny day after snow, when no one has showered, a roof leak or ice dam is more likely.

What's up in the attic?
A quick climb up the attic ladder with a flashlight will tell you a lot. Look for a flex duct sagging between joists, a duct that's come loose from the roof cap, or a duct that just ends in the attic.

Frost or water stains on the roof deck around it are a sign moist air is getting loose up there.

## Why Does the Duct Fill With Water?

Shower air is warm and nearly saturated. The attic in January is close to the outdoor temperature. When the air hits the cold duct walls, it cools past its dew point and the water comes out on the inside of the duct.

If the duct dips between joists, water collects in the low spot. If it runs uphill to a roof cap, the water runs back toward the fan. Either way, it ends up on your bathroom ceiling.

## How Do I Fix It?

Make sure it ends outside.
The DOE Building America Solution Center is clear: vent the fan directly outside, not into the attic, a crawlspace, the garage or a wall cavity. A duct that ends in the attic puts gallons of water into the attic every week.

Use smooth duct and keep it short.
Building America prefers smooth rigid duct, like galvanized metal or PVC, over flex. If you use flex, pull it tight so it doesn't sag.

Keep the first 2 to 3 feet straight off the fan, and use gentle bends instead of sharp elbows.

Insulate it.
Building America says to insulate the duct where it runs through the attic, and to bury it in the attic insulation. A warmer duct wall means less condensation inside.

Slope it to the outside.
Where you can, run the duct slightly downhill to a wall cap, so any water that forms drains out instead of back to the fan.

Check the damper.
The cap outside should have a damper that closes when the fan is off and opens freely when it runs. A stuck damper means the fan isn't moving much air, and a missing one lets cold air back in.

## Run It Long Enough

Most people shut the fan off as they leave the bathroom, while the room is still steamy. Minnesota Commerce suggests running it at least 15 minutes after use, and Building America recommends up to an hour. A timer switch does it for you.

Is my fan strong enough?
Building America cites at least 50 cfm for a fan that runs on demand, and notes installed airflow is often lower than the box says. A fan that can't hold a square of toilet paper against the grille is probably moving very little air.

## While You're Up There

The hole where the fan housing goes through the ceiling is one of the common attic air leaks. Seal the gap between the housing and the drywall from above.
