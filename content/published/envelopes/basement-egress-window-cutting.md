# Basement Egress Windows: What It Takes to Cut One Into a Midwest Foundation

> **Quick Summary:** If you want a bedroom in the basement, it needs a way out in a fire. That usually means cutting a larger opening into the foundation wall, installing a window big enough to climb through, and digging a window well outside. Get a permit, plan the support over the opening, control the concrete dust, and give the window well a drain, or the new window becomes the new leak.
>
> - Size requirements are local, but most Midwest cities follow the International Residential Code.
> - Cutting the foundation is structural work. Get a permit and plan the beam over the opening.
> - Wet-cut the concrete, and use a respirator indoors.
> - Drain the window well to the footing tile or a sump.

---

Finishing a basement is one of the best ways to add space to a Midwest house, and a bedroom down there needs an emergency exit. A small, high basement window doesn't count. In most cases, getting a legal bedroom means a new, larger window cut into the foundation.

## How Big Does the Opening Have to Be?

Egress rules are set by your city or county, and they vary. Most Midwest jurisdictions adopt the International Residential Code, which typically requires:

| Measurement | Typical requirement |
| :--- | :--- |
| Net clear opening | at least 5.7 sq ft |
| Clear opening height | at least 24 inches |
| Clear opening width | at least 20 inches |
| Height of the sill above the floor | no more than 44 inches |
| Window well area | at least 9 sq ft, at least 36 inches out from the wall |
| Ladder or steps | needed when the well is deeper than 44 inches |

"Net clear opening" is the actual space you can climb through with the window fully open, not the size of the glass or the frame. Casement windows usually give the most clear opening for the size of the hole. Confirm the numbers with your building department before ordering.

## Cutting the Foundation

Is the wall structural?
Yes. The foundation carries the house, and cutting a wider opening removes some of that support.

Most cities require a permit, and many require a steel or engineered beam, or a lintel, over the new opening. A structural engineer or your building department's approved details will set the size.

How is the cut made?
Contractors usually use a large walk-behind or wall saw with a diamond blade, cutting from both sides, then break out the piece. In block walls, the cores around the opening are filled with concrete and rebar to make solid jambs.

What about the dust?
Concrete dust contains crystalline silica, which damages lungs. OSHA's guidance for walk-behind saws calls for a continuous stream of water on the blade, enough to "minimize the release of visible dust."

Outdoors, wet cutting with these saws doesn't require a respirator under OSHA's table. Indoors or in enclosed spaces, it requires a respirator with an assigned protection factor of at least 10.

## Keeping the Window Well Dry

A new window well is a hole next to your foundation that collects rain and snowmelt. If it doesn't drain, water pools against the new window and finds its way in.

Where does the water go?
Put a layer of gravel at the bottom of the well and a drain that connects down to the footing drain tile or to a sump. Building Science Corporation's basement guide (BSD-103) describes the same principle for the whole foundation: "drain the site and drain the ground."

How do I keep water out of it?
Slope the ground around the well away from it, keep downspouts away, and consider a cover that lets light through but sheds rain and snow. A cover must still open easily from inside for escape.

## Finishing Around the New Window

Seal the gap between the window frame and the concrete with backer rod and sealant or low-expansion foam, and flash the opening so water that gets behind the trim drains out. Insulate the basement walls around it with rigid foam against the concrete rather than fiberglass, since basements are rarely bone-dry.
