# Cold Upstairs, Ice Dams Outside: Air Sealing and Insulating Knee Walls in 1.5-Story Homes

> **Quick Summary:** In a Cape Cod, bungalow or other 1.5-story house, the upstairs rooms are tucked under the roof, with short knee walls and small attic spaces behind them. Those walls often have little insulation and nothing stopping air on the attic side, and the floor joists under them are open to the cold. Air flows straight under your upstairs floor. Building America lays out two fixes: seal and insulate the knee wall and block the joists, or move the insulation up to the roof.
>
> - Block the open floor joist bays under the knee wall. That's usually the biggest leak.
> - Put a rigid air barrier on the attic side of the knee wall.
> - Or move insulation and air sealing up to the roofline.
> - Fixing these spaces also helps with ice dams.

---

If your upstairs bedroom is freezing in January and stuffy in July, and you get ice along the bottom of the roof every winter, the knee walls are a good place to look. They are one of the most common trouble spots in older Midwest homes.

## What's Behind the Knee Wall?

Open the little access door in the knee wall and you'll see a low, triangle-shaped attic running along the side of the house. It's outside the heated part of the home, just like the main attic.

Where is it leaking?
Usually in three places. The knee wall itself often has batts with no covering on the attic side, so air moves right through them.

The floor joists run under the knee wall from the triangle space into the space between your upstairs floor and the ceiling below, with nothing blocking them. And the sloped ceiling above the room may have thin or missing insulation.

That open joist space is the big one. Cold air from the attic flows into the floor of your upstairs rooms, the same way wind finds the gap under a door.

## Why Does It Cause Ice Dams?

Heat escaping into those spaces warms the underside of the roof. The Building America Solution Center notes this melts snow and contributes to ice dams in cold climates, and the University of Minnesota names knee wall spaces as one of the heat sources behind them.

## Option 1: Seal and Insulate the Knee Wall

This keeps the triangle space outside the house and treats the knee wall like an exterior wall.

Block the joist bays.
Under the knee wall, close off each joist space between the attic and the floor. Building America suggests solid wood blocking or rigid board caulked in place, or batts stuffed in and covered with spray foam.

Cover the attic side.
Put a rigid air barrier, such as rigid foam board, drywall or plywood, on the attic side of the knee wall studs, and seal the seams and edges with caulk, foam or tape. That keeps air from moving through the insulation.

Insulate the floor of the triangle space.
Then insulate the attic floor out to the eaves, the same way you would a main attic.

## Option 2: Move the Boundary to the Roof

This brings the triangle space inside the heated part of the house. Building America describes running the insulation and a continuous air barrier along the underside of the roof, from the top of the knee wall down to the top of the exterior wall.

When does this make sense?
When there are ducts, pipes or storage back there you'd like inside, or when access to the knee wall itself is tough. It means insulating the slope instead of the floor, so it's often more work and may need foam to get enough insulation in the rafter depth.

## Do It in Order

Seal the air leaks first, then insulate. Insulation slows heat, but air moving through it carries heat right past it. Building America points out that spray foam can serve as the air barrier if it's thick enough, at least 1.5 inches of closed-cell or 5.5 inches of open-cell.

Heating an upstairs that leaks into the roof costs money every day it's cold, and those dollars are going to melt snow.

Start by opening the knee wall access door with a flashlight and looking at the floor joists. If you can see into the space between the joists, that's your first job. You can do it yourself if you're comfortable crawling, or have an insulation contractor quote both options.
