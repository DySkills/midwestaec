# How to Insulate a Solid Brick Wall From the Inside Without Ruining It

> **Quick Summary:** In an older brick house the walls are two or three layers of solid brick, and heat leaking out from the house is part of what keeps them dry. Insulate from the inside and the brick gets colder and stays wetter, which is how freeze-thaw damage starts. It can be done safely, but only in the right order: stop the water, test the brick, protect the joist ends, then add airtight insulation tight to the wall.
>
> - If you can't fix the water getting into the wall, don't insulate it from the inside.
> - Repoint with mortar that is softer than the brick.
> - Use airtight insulation pressed against the brick, not batts with an air gap.
> - Watch the joist ends and check on the wall for a few winters afterward.

---

If you live in a brick two-flat in Chicago or a brick foursquare in St. Louis, the walls are probably solid masonry, not brick veneer over a wood frame. They feel cold in winter because there's no insulation, just about R-5 of brick (Straube, Ueno and Schumacher, *Measure Guideline: Internal Insulation of Masonry Walls*, 2012).

Adding insulation inside is the obvious fix, and it does work. But it changes how the wall behaves.

Before, heat leaking from the house kept the brick warmer and helped it dry. After, the brick sits closer to outdoor temperature and stays wet longer, and water freezing inside wet brick is what pops the faces off.

## Before You Start

Should this house be insulated from the inside at all?
The National Park Service's guidance on historic buildings (*Preservation Brief 3*) puts air sealing, the attic and the basement first, and wall insulation after them. It also says interior insulation on historic masonry should be avoided where it would cover important interior features. If you haven't sealed the attic and basement yet, start there.

Is it safe to work on?
Raking out mortar makes silica dust, and old plaster and paint may hold lead. Wear a P100 respirator and use tools with HEPA dust collection. Contractors have to follow OSHA's silica rule and, in pre-1978 homes, EPA's lead-safe work rules.

## Step 1: Stop the Water First

This is the step that decides everything else. The Building America measure guideline says it plainly: "If rain control cannot be addressed and upgraded, interior insulation should not be implemented." Building Science Corporation calls controlling surface water "the most critical aspect of controlling the moisture content of the masonry" (Straube, BSD-114).

Walk the outside of the house. Gutters and downspouts should carry water away from the wall, not dump it down the face. Window sills should slope out and drip clear of the brick, and the guideline calls for drip edges that project at least half an inch past the wall below.

Look for kickout flashing where a roof meets a wall, and for brick that sits in contact with soil.

## Step 2: Repoint With Soft Mortar

Open or crumbling joints let rain straight into the wall. Repointing fixes that, but the mortar has to be right. The National Park Service's repointing guidance (*Preservation Brief 2*) says "the new mortar must have greater vapor permeability and be softer (measured in compressive strength) than the masonry units," so any stress and moisture goes through the joint, which is easy to replace, rather than through the brick.

That rules out hard, high-Portland mixes on most pre-1930s brick. Ask for a high-lime mortar matched to your brick, such as a Type O or a natural hydraulic lime mix. The brief calls for raking out old mortar to 2 to 2.5 times the joint width and packing the new mortar in layers about a quarter inch thick.

## Step 3: Find Out How Tough Your Brick Is

Not all old brick handles cold the same way. Some was fired hard and shrugs off freezing, while softer brick soaks up water and breaks apart. The Building America guideline lays out a step-by-step assessment, starting with a site visit for existing damage and leaks, then lab tests of brick samples, including how saturated the brick can get before freezing damages it.

A RILEM tube, a small glass tube stuck to the brick face, is a cheap field check of how fast the brick soaks up water. It's a useful first look, but it doesn't replace lab testing. For a large or valuable house, a masonry consultant or a building science lab can test a few brick samples and model the wall before you commit.

Check where your house sits, too. Driving rain is "the largest load" on the wall (Straube and Schumacher, 2012), so a house on a lake-effect shore, or a wall facing the prevailing storms, needs more caution than a sheltered one. GLISA puts extra winter precipitation at about 40 percent in Lake Michigan's lake-effect zone and about 100 percent downwind of Lake Superior.

## Step 4: Protect the Joist Ends

In most solid brick houses, the floor joists sit in pockets in the wall. Once the wall is insulated, those joist ends get colder, and research still shows "substantial uncertainty" about how they hold up afterward (Building America measure guideline).

Check the moisture content of the joist ends with a pin meter. A USDA Forest Products Laboratory review (Carll and Highley, 1999) found decay usually needs wood moisture above about 25 to 30 percent, so readings under 20 percent leave a working margin.

Seal air leaks around each joist with caulk or foam so warm, moist house air can't reach the cold pocket, and consider borate rods drilled into the joist ends. BSD-114 also suggests using thinner insulation right at the joists so a little heat still reaches them.

## Step 5: Insulate Tight to the Brick

Air gaps are the problem here. BSD-114 warns that a gap behind the insulation lets air loop through it, cutting the R-value and encouraging condensation on the cold brick. That's why a regular stud wall with batts and drywall, set an inch off the brick, is not recommended.

Which kind of insulation?
North American cold-climate research favors airtight insulation tight to the masonry, and BSD-114 calls closed-cell spray foam sprayed onto the brick "the most common successful strategy." European research on old brick, much of it in Denmark and Belgium, favors vapor-open boards such as calcium silicate, which let the wall dry toward the room. Both need full contact with the brick, so pick one approach and have it detailed by someone who has done it before.

How much does it save?
The Building America guideline modeled a typical case where 1.5 inches of closed-cell foam cut heat flow through the wall by about 60 percent, and 3 inches by about 75 percent. More insulation also makes the brick colder, so on the wettest, most exposed walls a thinner layer can be the safer choice.

Where do the studs go?
With spray foam, hold any new studs 1 to 2 inches off the brick so the foam fills in behind them. With boards, set the boards tight to the brick first and build the stud wall inside them.

## Step 6: Keep It Airtight and Watch It

The room side of the wall needs to be airtight, so moist house air can't leak into the wall and condense on the cold brick. Taped drywall or a sealed membrane does that job.

You usually don't need a plastic vapor barrier. BSD-114 says a barrier under 1 perm is not needed in most cases, though vapor control matters more when indoor humidity stays above about 30 to 40 percent in winter.

Then keep an eye on it. *Preservation Brief 3* recommends periodic monitoring of insulated masonry walls. Look at the brick faces each spring for flaking, and check the joist ends once a winter for the first few years.
