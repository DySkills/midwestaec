# How to Know If You Need an ICF Basement: Signs, Options and When to Call an Engineer

> **Quick Summary:** If you're searching for ICF because your basement is cold, damp or cracking, you probably don't need a new foundation. Most of those problems come from water around the house and missing insulation, and they're fixed with drainage and foam against the existing wall. A few signs, like a bowing wall or a long horizontal crack, are structural and need an engineer. ICF makes the most sense when a foundation is being built anyway.
>
> - White powder and musty smells point to water, not a failed foundation.
> - Bowing walls and long horizontal cracks need a structural engineer.
> - Cold, damp walls are usually fixed with foam board inside the wall.
> - ICF fits new houses and additions best.

---

ICF foundations get a lot of attention, and for good reason: the foam is built into both sides of the wall. But a lot of people looking at ICF are really trying to solve a problem with a basement they already have. Here's how to read what your basement is telling you.

## What Do the Signs Mean?

White powder on the wall?
That's efflorescence, the minerals water leaves behind when it moves through concrete and evaporates on the inside. It means water is getting into the wall. Building Science Corporation's basement guide (BSD-103) explains that wet soil wicks into foundations by capillary action.

The fix is outside: grading, downspouts and drainage.

A musty smell in summer?
Basement walls and floors stay cool, and BSD-103 names the main summer risk: "warm moist air comes in contact with basement cold surfaces." If the walls are covered with fiberglass and plastic, moisture can collect behind them where you can't see it. Pull back a corner of any finished wall and look.

Water at the wall-floor joint after rain?
NDSU Extension notes that's the usual way in when soil around the house is saturated and the drain tile is missing or not working (NDSU DE1570). It calls for drainage, not a new wall.

## Which Signs Need an Engineer?

Is a wall bowing in?
Hang a string or use a long level along the wall from top to bottom. A wall that bows inward is being pushed by the soil outside, and saturated or frozen soil pushes harder.

Is there a long horizontal crack?
Vertical hairline cracks from concrete shrinking are common and usually not structural. A horizontal crack running along much of a wall, or stair-step cracks in a block wall, often go with bowing.

Is the wall moving?
Mark the ends of a crack with a pencil and a date, and check again in a month. Cracks that are growing, or walls that are moving, need attention soon.

If you see any of these, have a licensed structural engineer look at the wall before you get repair quotes. The engineer's job is to tell you what's wrong. A contractor's job is to sell you a fix, and the two aren't always the same.

## What Are the Options?

For a cold, damp basement you're finishing?
BSD-103 and the DOE Building America Solution Center recommend rigid foam or closed-cell spray foam set directly against the concrete, with no air gap. BSD-103 gives examples like 2 inches of unfaced XPS foam for about R-10, or 3 inches of closed-cell spray foam for about R-18.

For a wall that's moving?
An engineer may recommend anchors, braces, carbon-fiber straps or rebuilding part of the wall. ICF is one way to rebuild, but it's rarely needed for a single damaged wall.

For a new house or addition?
That's where ICF shines. A DOE Building America cold-climate test house measured R-22 ICF foundation walls and very tight air sealing, at about $2,500 more than a framed R-13 wall (Mallay and Wiehagen, 2014).

## Planning a Living Space Down There?

If you want a bedroom in the basement, you'll need a proper emergency exit, which usually means an egress window, along with dry walls and floors. Plan the window, the drainage and the insulation together, so you aren't cutting into a newly finished wall later.
