# Midwest Ice Dams: Why They Form, What to Do Today, and Why a New Roof Rarely Fixes Them

> **Quick Summary:** An ice dam forms when heat from your house melts the snow on the roof, and the water refreezes at the cold edge. The ridge of ice holds back more meltwater, which works under the shingles and into the walls. Today, get the snow off from the ground with a roof rake. For good, seal the air leaks into the attic, then add insulation. Heat cables and a new roof don't fix the heat leak.
>
> - Ice dams need snow, a warm roof and a cold eave.
> - Warm air leaking into the attic is a major source of the heat.
> - Rake the snow from the ground. Don't climb up or chip the ice.
> - Seal the attic first, then insulate.

---

After a big snow and a week of cold, the icicles along your gutters keep getting longer. Then a water stain shows up at the top of a bedroom wall, or water drips around a window frame. Building Science Corporation's ice dam digest (Straube, BSD-135) notes they're most likely where snowfall is heavy and the cold lasts "at least a month," which describes most Midwest winters.

## How Does an Ice Dam Form?

Three things have to line up: snow on the roof, a roof deck warm enough to melt it, and a cold eave where the water can freeze. The warm part of the roof is over the heated house. The eave hangs out past the walls, over cold air, so it stays below freezing.

Snow melts on the upper roof, the water runs down under the snow, and it freezes when it reaches the eave. Each day the ridge of ice gets taller and holds back more water. Shingles are made to shed water running downhill, not to hold back a pond, so the water backs up under them.

## Where Does the Heat Come From?

Air leaks, mostly. University of Minnesota Extension's ice dam guide (Huelman, Stone, Larson and Hendricks) says that in many homes, warm air leaking through ceiling penetrations is a major source of the heat behind ice dams. BSD-135 lists the usual paths: plumbing stacks, recessed lights, chimneys and attic hatches.

Is there ductwork in the attic?
BSD-135 also names ducts, hot water pipes and other heat sources above the ceiling insulation. Leaky ducts in an attic blow warm air straight onto the underside of the roof.

Is the insulation thin at the edges?
The insulation is usually thinnest right over the outside walls, where the roof slopes down. Heat leaks through there and warms the lower roof, close to the eave where the dam forms.

Is the sun part of it?
On sunny days, BSD-135 notes, exposed shingles can heat up as much as 70°F above the air, melting snow next to them even on a well-insulated roof. That's why some small ice dams happen on almost any roof.

## What Should I Do Today?

Can I get the snow off safely?
UMN Extension suggests a roof rake or push broom to clear snow, which takes away the water supply, while noting it can damage the roofing. Work from the ground. Building Science Corporation's Joseph Lstiburek warns that people are hurt every year by falling ice and falls from roofs (BSI-097).

Water is coming into the house. Now what?
In an emergency, UMN Extension suggests making channels through the dam with tap water on a warm day, starting at the bottom edge and working up. The channel will stop working "within days," but it can drain the pond behind the dam for now.

Should I chip the ice off?
No. Lstiburek's photos of roofs after people hacked at ice dams show the damage it does to shingles and gutters. If the dam is large or the leak is bad, hire a crew that removes ice with steam.

## How Do I Stop It From Coming Back?

UMN Extension's order of work is to air-seal the ceiling first, so warm, moist air can't reach the attic, and then add insulation to cut the heat that conducts through. BSD-135 agrees, and adds a warning: ventilating a leaky attic to remove the heat is "very difficult and bad practise," and adding vents to a leaky ceiling just gives the leaking air an easier way out.

What about heat cables?
The Minnesota Department of Commerce's *Home Energy Guide* (July 2025) advises against relying on heat cables or adding roof vents, and names attic air leaks as the primary cause of ice dams. Cables melt a path at the eave, but the heat leak that feeds the dam is still there.

What about the roof itself?
When you reroof, a self-adhered ice and water membrane at the eaves helps keep backed-up water out. DOE's Building America Solution Center recommends it run more than 2 feet up the roof past the inside of the wall in climate zone 5 and colder. It protects the house, but it doesn't stop the dam.

## When to Call Someone

If you have ice dams every winter, a weatherization or insulation contractor with a blower door and an infrared camera can find the attic leaks in an afternoon. Ask them to seal before they insulate, and to test before and after. If water has gotten into the walls, have someone check the insulation and framing behind the stain for wet or moldy material.
