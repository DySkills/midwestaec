# Roof Heat Cables for Ice Dams: When They Help, What They Cost, and the Fire Risk

> **Quick Summary:** A roof heat cable is a heated wire laid in a zigzag along the eaves and down through the gutters and downspouts. It melts a narrow path through the ice so water can drain off instead of backing up under the shingles. It doesn't fix what causes ice dams, which is heat leaking into the attic. It also runs on electricity for as long as it's on, and the wrong kind can start a fire. It can be a reasonable stopgap on a roof you can't fix easily.
>
> - Heat cables drain water through ice dams; they don't stop the ice from forming.
> - Use only cables listed for roofs and gutters, never pipe heat tape.
> - Put them on a GFCI outlet and a thermostat or snow sensor.
> - Air sealing the attic fixes the cause.

---

If you've had water in the house from an ice dam, a heat cable can look like the easy answer. Hang it, plug it in, done. It can help, but it's worth knowing what it does and doesn't do before you buy one.

## How Does a Heat Cable Work?

It's a resistance heater, the same idea as a toaster element, turned into a long flexible wire. Laid along the eaves in a zigzag and run through the gutter and downspout, it keeps a narrow strip warm enough that water can flow through the ice and off the roof.

Does it stop ice dams?
Not really. The ice still forms around and above the cable.

What the cable does is keep a drain path open, so the water behind the dam has somewhere to go besides under your shingles.

## Why Doesn't It Fix the Problem?

Ice dams start higher up the roof. Heat leaking from the house warms the roof deck, snow melts there, and the water freezes when it reaches the cold eaves. Building Science Corporation's ice dam digest (Straube, BSD-135) describes the cure as keeping the deck cold, which means stopping that heat.

The University of Minnesota points to air leaks into the attic as the main source in many homes. A heat cable adds heat at the bottom of the roof but does nothing about the heat coming in at the top.

## What Does It Cost to Run?

You can work it out from the label.
Multiply the watts per foot by the length to get the cable's power, then by the hours it's on. A 100-foot cable rated at 5 watts per foot draws 500 watts.

Left on around the clock, that's 12 kilowatt-hours a day, and it adds up fast across a winter.

How do I cut that?
Use a control. A thermostat that only turns the cable on near freezing, or a snow and moisture sensor, keeps it from running on cold, dry days when there's nothing to melt.

Paying for heat is already pricey and getting pricier. A cable is heat on purpose, on top of the heat the attic is already leaking.

## Is It a Fire Risk?

It can be. The U.S.

Consumer Product Safety Commission estimated that faulty electric heat tapes cause about 1,500 fires, 10 deaths and 100 injuries each year. Most of its rules apply to any heated cable.

What does CPSC recommend?
Plug it into a grounded, GFCI-protected outlet. Don't wrap the cable over itself unless the instructions say you can, and keep the end cap sealed and dry.

Inspect it every year and replace it if you see charring, discoloration at the plug, cuts or bare wire.

Which cable should I use?
CPSC is clear that heat tape made for water pipes should not be used on gutters. Buy a cable listed for roof and gutter de-icing, and follow its clip and spacing instructions so it isn't damaging the shingles.

## When Does a Heat Cable Make Sense?

Some roofs are hard to fix from the inside. A low-slope porch roof, a valley over a cathedral ceiling, or a 1.5-story house with no room to insulate are common examples. A cable can keep water draining there while you plan the real fix.

For most attics, sealing the air leaks and adding insulation is the better use of the same money. It stops the ice and cuts your heating bill at the same time.

If you already have cables up, check them before the first storm: look over the plug and the run along the eaves, and make sure it's on a GFCI outlet. You can do it yourself, or have an electrician or roofer look while they're up there.
